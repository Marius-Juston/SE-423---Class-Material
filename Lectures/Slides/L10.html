Lecture notes

This slide is text only, with no figures. It states that devices can exchange data in two ways, parallel communication or serial communication, and asks why we would not simply use parallel. It gives two reasons. First, parallel needs many I/O pins, and pins on a microcontroller are already very limited. Second, running many wires side by side invites electromagnetic interference, or EMI, and cross-talk. Serial communication instead serializes the data into a single-bit stream and needs much less circuit complexity. The slide then asks what the trade-off is, answers "Time!", and closes with: serializing an N-bit word requires N discrete clock cycles.

In parallel communication, every bit of a word has its own wire, so a 16-bit value needs 16 data lines plus control lines, and the whole word arrives in one clock cycle. In serial communication, those 16 bits go one after another down a single data line, so the same word takes 16 clock cycles. You trade pins and wiring for transfer time. On the Red Board this trade is almost always worth it. The F28379D's pins are shared among PWM, ADC, encoder, and GPIO functions. Serial peripherals like SCI (UART) and SPI let you reach sensors, DACs, and the Raspberry Pi with just two to four wires.

Cross-talk means a signal on one wire induces an unwanted signal on a neighboring wire. That idea leads directly into the next slides, which look at how interference couples between conductors.

Quick check: if your SPI clock runs at 1 MHz, how long does it take to send one 16-bit word? The answer is 16 microseconds.

7. Serial Communication - Interferences

There are 4 interference couplings:

  • Conductive: You have physical connection causing interference
  • Inductive: source and victim are separated by a short distance (typically less than a wavelength). Can be of two kinds, electrical induction and magnetic induction.
    • It is common to refer to electrical induction as capacitive coupling, and to magnetic induction as inductive coupling.
  • Capacitive: occurs when a varying electrical field exists between two adjacent conductors, inducing a change in voltage on the receiving conductor.
  • Inductive: magnetic coupling occurs when a varying magnetic field exists between two parallel conductors, inducing a change in voltage along the receiving conductor.

8. Serial Communication – Interferences - Capacitive

Let’s try to explain how we get capacitive interference!

Let V1(t)V_{1}(t) be the time-varying voltage on the noisy source wires and V2(t)V_{2}(t) be the voltage on the victim wire. Let C12C_{12} be the mutual stray capacitance between the two wires (could be air), let the victim wire have a resistance of RR.

The fundamental equation for current through a capacitor is:

I=CdVdtI = C\frac{dV}{dt}

The current injected from wire 1 into wire 2, InoiseI_{noise}, is driven from the difference between them across the capacitor.

Inoise=C12d(V1−V2)dtI_{noise} = C_{12}\frac{d(V_{1} - V_{2})}{dt}

In practice, the noise source voltage is much larger than the victim’s wire (V1≫V2V_{1} \gg V_{2}), which allows the voltage derivative to simplify to:

Inoise=C12d(V1−V2)dt≈C12dV1dtI_{noise} = C_{12}\frac{d(V_{1} - V_{2})}{dt} \approx C_{12}\frac{dV_{1}}{dt}

Lecture notes

This slide derives capacitive interference from the capacitor equation. It is all text and equations, with no figure. In the last slide we listed the four coupling types; now we look at capacitive coupling in detail.

The slide sets up the variables. V1(t) is the time-varying voltage on the noisy source wire. V2(t) is the voltage on the victim wire. C12 is the mutual stray capacitance between the two wires. The victim wire has a resistance R. "Stray" means nobody put a capacitor there on purpose. Any two conductors near each other, with air or insulation between them acting as the dielectric, form a small capacitor.

Next comes the fundamental capacitor equation: current equals capacitance times the time derivative of voltage, I = C*dV/dt. A capacitor only passes current when the voltage across it is changing.

The voltage across the stray capacitor is the difference between the two wires. So the noise current injected from wire 1 into wire 2 is I_noise = C12*d(V1 - V2)/dt.

In practice the noise source voltage is much larger than the victim's voltage, V1 >> V2. You can then drop V2, which gives I_noise ≈ C12*dV1/dt.

The key insight is that the noise current depends on how fast V1 changes, not on how big it is. A modest voltage with sharp edges, like a fast PWM or clock line, can inject more noise than a large but slow signal.

Quick check: does doubling the distance between the wires increase or decrease I_noise? It decreases, because C12 shrinks.

On the next slide, this current flows through the victim's resistance R and becomes a noise voltage.

This slide finishes the capacitive coupling derivation from the previous slide and turns it into a practical rule.

The text says that, by Ohm's law, the injected noise current flows through the victim wire's impedance to ground, R, and creates a noise voltage. The equation reads: V_noise is approximately I_noise times R, which is approximately R times C_12 times dV_1/dt. In words, the noise voltage is the victim's resistance times the mutual stray capacitance times the rate of change of the source voltage. The slide stresses that V_noise is directly proportional to the rate of change of the source voltage, even when the voltage amplitude itself is moderate.

The slide then asks: what causes more digital signal interference, high-frequency or low-frequency? A red box highlights "High-frequency." It then asks which of two PWM settings is worse, assuming a 50% duty cycle: EPwm7Regs.TBPRD = 5000 or EPwm7Regs.TBPRD = 500. A red box highlights TBPRD = 500.

Here is why. TBPRD sets the PWM period in time-base clock counts, so a smaller TBPRD gives a shorter period and a higher frequency. At 500 counts the period is about one tenth as long, so the frequency is about ten times higher. Each switching edge has a large dV/dt and injects a pulse of noise current. A ten-times-faster PWM produces ten times as many edges per second, and for sinusoidal components dV/dt itself scales with frequency.

A common mistake is to assume that a 3.3 V signal is too small to cause trouble. It is the fast edges, not the amplitude, that couple noise. On your robot, keep fast PWM motor wires away from sensor and SPI lines.

10. Serial Communication – Interferences - Inductive (1/4)

Let’s try to explain how we get inductive interference!

Coupling between the circuits can occur when the magnetic field lines from one of the circuits pass through the loop formed by the other circuit.

Magnetic coupling is governed by Ampere's Law and Faraday's Law of Induction. A time-varying current in a source wire generates a time-varying magnetic field, which penetrates the loop area formed by the victim wire and its ground return path, inducing an electromotive force (EMF).

Let I1(t)I_{1}(t) be the time-varying current for. Assuming it is a long, straight wire, we use Ampere’s Law (∮𝑩⋅𝕕𝒍=μ0Ienc\oint_{}^{}{{\mathbf{B}} \cdot \mathbb{d}{\mathbf{l}}} = \mu_{0}I_{enc}) to find the magnetic field at radial distance rr:

B(r,t)=μ0I1(t)2πrϕ̂B(r,t) = \frac{\mu_{0}I_{1}(t)}{2\pi r}\widehat{\phi}

(there μ0\mu_{0}, is the permeability of free space and ϕ̂\widehat{\phi} represents the flux)

Lecture notes

This slide moves from capacitive coupling to inductive (magnetic) coupling. Capacitive coupling depends on how fast the source voltage changes. Inductive coupling depends on how fast the source current changes.

The figure shows two circuits above a shared grounded conducting plane. Each circuit has an AC voltage source and a source resistor at one end, a long wire running parallel to the plane, and a load resistor at the far end that returns to the plane. A circular magnetic field, labelled H, wraps around the upper circuit's wire, with arrows showing its direction. Those field lines pass through the loop formed by the neighboring wire and the plane. That loop is the key: the victim wire plus its ground return path encloses an area, and magnetic flux through that area couples the two circuits.

Two laws govern this. Ampere's Law says a current creates a magnetic field. Faraday's Law says a changing magnetic flux through a loop induces an electromotive force, or EMF, which is a voltage driving current around that loop.

Let I_1(t) be the time-varying current in the source wire. For a long, straight wire, Ampere's Law, the closed line integral of B dot dl = mu_0 * I_enc, gives the field at radial distance r:

B(r,t) = (mu_0 * I_1(t)) / (2*pi*r) in the phi-hat direction.

Here mu_0 is the permeability of free space. The slide says phi-hat "represents the flux". More precisely, phi-hat is the azimuthal unit vector, the direction that circles around the wire, and the field in that direction is what produces flux through the victim loop. Notice that the field falls off as 1/r, so distance between wires helps.

Next, you integrate this field over the victim loop's area to get the flux.

This slide continues the list of communication peripherals on the Red Board's TMS320F28379D. The previous slide covered SCI, SPI and I2C. This one lists four more: CAN, McBSP, USB and uPP, each with a one-line description.

CAN, the Controller Area Network, is a robust bus built for cars. It uses differential signaling: the information is carried as the voltage difference between two wires. This slide lists the SPI module's interrupt signals and its connection to the DMA controller. These are the lines you saw leaving the SPI block in the diagram on the previous slide.

The slide says the SPI has two interrupt signals, then lists three names, because one physical line has two names. In non-FIFO mode the first line is called SPIINT and fires for both transmit and receive events, once per word. In FIFO mode the same line is called SPIRXINT and fires on receive events. A FIFO is a first-in, first-out buffer. With the FIFO enabled, the SPI can queue several words. You choose how many words arrive before SPIRXINT fires, or how few remain before SPITXINT asks for more. The CPU then handles a batch of words in one interrupt instead of one interrupt per word.

The DMA, or direct memory access controller, goes one step further. It moves data between the SPI and memory with no CPU instructions at all. The SPI requests a transfer with SPITXDMA, a transmit request to the DMA, or SPIRXDMA, a receive request to the DMA. There are two limits: DMA transfers are 16-bit register reads and writes only, and they work only when the FIFO is enabled.

A common mistake is enabling FIFO mode and then waiting on SPIINT behavior. Check which mode you configured before writing your interrupt service routine.

Lecture notes

This slide is text only, with no figures. It introduces 3-wire SPI mode on the F28379D: setting the TRIWIRE bit to 1 lets the SPI run over three pins instead of the usual four.

44. Serial Communication – I2C (1/12)

The I2C bus consists of 2 wires: Serial Data (SDA) and Serial Clock (SCL). Instead of relying active push-pull transistors like SPI (which forces a line high or low), I2C uses an open-draw architecture with passive pull-up resistors.

When a microcontroller wants to send a logical 1, it simply “lets go” of the wire, and the physical pull-up resistor pulls the voltage to VccV_{cc} (e.g., 3.3V). When it wants to send a logical 0, an internal transistor connects the wire to ground.

Why?

If 2 devices accidentally talk at the exact same time, and one sends a 0 (pulls to ground), while the other sends a 1 (let’s go), the line safely goes to ground. There is no short circuit!

Lecture notes

This slide introduces how I2C works at the electrical level. It is all text, with no figures. It says the I2C bus uses just two wires: Serial Data, SDA, and Serial Clock, SCL. SPI uses active push-pull drivers that force a line high or low. I2C instead uses an open-drain architecture with passive pull-up resistors. The slide spells it "open-draw", but the standard term is open-drain, named after the drain terminal of the output transistor.

To send a logical 1, a device simply "lets go" of the wire, and the pull-up resistor pulls the line up to V_cc, for example 3.3 V. To send a logical 0, an internal transistor connects the wire to ground. The slide then asks "Why?"

The answer is safety when devices collide. Suppose two devices talk at the same instant, and one pulls the line low while the other releases it. The line simply goes to ground. There is no short circuit, because only the resistor is sourcing current.

Compare that with two push-pull outputs, one driving high and one driving low. That connects V_cc almost directly to ground through two transistors, which can damage the pins. This behavior is often called "wired-AND": the line is high only if every device releases it.

Recall the question from two slides ago about talking to 15 temperature sensors. With SPI, each sensor would need its own chip-select line. Open-drain is what lets many devices share the same two wires safely.

A common mistake is forgetting the pull-up resistors. Without them, nothing ever pulls the line high, and the bus never reads a 1.

47. Serial Communication – I2C (4/12)

Because there is no Chip Select wires, the Master must should a specific “Address” onto the bus. Every slave device listens. The protocol is strictly framed as:

  • START condition: The Master pulls SDA low while SCL remains high. This violates normal data rules, acting as a wake-up for all devices.
  • Addressing: The Master transmits a 7-bit (or 10-bit) hardware addres, folled by a Read/Write direction bit.
  • Acknowledge (ACK): The specific Slave that matches the address pulls the SDA line low on the 9th clock cycle to say “I am here”
  • Data Transfer: Butes are clocked across the SDA line
  • STOP Condition: The Master releases SDA while SCL is high, signaling the bus is free.
Lecture notes

This slide lists, in order, the five parts of every I2C transaction. Because I2C has no chip select wires, the master cannot pick a device by pulling a dedicated line low the way it does with SPISTE in SPI. Instead it puts a specific address onto the shared bus, and every slave listens and compares that address to its own.

First is the START condition: the master pulls SDA, the data line, low while SCL, the clock line, stays high. Normally SDA may change only while SCL is low, so an SDA edge while SCL is high breaks that rule on purpose. That is how every device recognizes a START and wakes up.

Next is addressing: the master sends a 7-bit address (or a 10-bit one), followed by a Read/Write direction bit. Seven bits allow 2^7 = 128 possible addresses.

Then comes the acknowledge, or ACK. On the 9th clock cycle, only the slave whose address matched pulls SDA low, which says "I am here." If no device answers, the pullup resistor leaves SDA high, and that is a no-acknowledge.

After that, data bytes are clocked across SDA, and each byte gets its own acknowledge.

Finally, the STOP condition: the master releases SDA so it rises while SCL is high, which signals that the bus is free.

A common mistake is address format. Many datasheets list an 8-bit value that already includes the R/W bit, while the F28379D's I2C module expects the 7-bit address. If no device ever acknowledges, check this first.

Lecture notes

This slide introduces multi-master arbitration on I2C. It has four statements and no figure. First, I2C lets several microcontrollers share the same two wires, and two of them may try to issue a START condition at the same moment. Second, both masters clock out their addresses bit by bit while monitoring the actual voltage on SDA, the data line. Having covered 7-bit and 10-bit addressing, we now ask what happens when two devices both want to be master. The answer comes from the open-drain wiring. No device ever drives SDA high. A pull-up resistor holds the line high, and any device can only pull it low. This makes the bus a wired-AND: if anyone outputs 0, the line reads 0. So a master sending 0 never sees a conflict, and only the master sending 1 can detect one. That is why the lower address wins arbitration.

Arbitration is non-destructive. The winner never notices the contest, so no time or data is lost. A common mistake is to think a push-pull output would work the same way. It would not: a high and a low output fighting each other is a short circuit.

The arrow labeled "Device #2 drives SDA" shows that device 2 finishes its full sequence, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0, 1, and SDA carries exactly that.

The key point is that this arbitration is lossless. Device 2 never knows there was a conflict, and its packet is not corrupted. As a consequence, the master sending the first 0 where the other sends a 1 wins. A message with a numerically lower address therefore gets priority.

Quick check: what would happen if device 1 drove the line actively high instead of releasing it? You would get a short circuit and garbage on the bus.

The same wired-AND idea also governs the clock, which is the question on the next slide.

53. If you have 2 masters sending Serial Clock (SCL) signals what is a problem?

Lecture notes

This slide is a discussion question in large white text on the dark blue title background: "If you have 2 masters sending Serial Clock (SCL) signals, what is a problem?"

The arbitration you just saw on SDA quietly assumed both masters share one clock, so each bit is compared at the same moment. But each master generates SCL from its own internal timer, with its own frequency and phase. Their edges will not line up, so who decides when a bit is valid?

So the question for you is: how do we get the electrical signals to agree on a single clock wave? Think about how the open-drain lines behave, which the next slides explain.

54. Serial Communication – I2C (10/12)

The microcontrollers on the bus can only drive the SCL line as such:

  • Drive LOW: They can turn on an internal transistor to connect the SCL line to ground (0V)
  • Release HIGH: They can turn off the transistor, letting go of the line. They cannot force the line high; they rely entirely on the pull-up resistor to pull the voltage back up

Because of this, the SCL line acts as a massive physical AND gate.

  • The SCL line will only transition HIGH if ALL devices let go of it.
  • The SCL line will instantly transition LOW if ANY device pulls it to ground.
Lecture notes

This slide answers the question from the previous slide: what happens when two masters both try to drive the Serial Clock line, SCL? The text explains how any device on the bus is physically allowed to drive SCL, and what that means for the voltage on the wire.

Each device has only two options. It can drive the line LOW by turning on an internal transistor that connects SCL to ground, 0 V. Or it can release the line by turning that transistor off, letting go of the wire. A device can never force the line high. The only thing that pulls SCL back up is the external pull-up resistor. This arrangement is called open-drain (or open-collector) output.

Because of this, SCL behaves as one big physical AND gate, called a wired-AND. The line goes HIGH only if ALL devices have released it, and it goes LOW the instant ANY device pulls it to ground. In logic form, SCL = device1 AND device2 AND ... AND deviceN, where each device's term is 1 when it has released the line.

This is why two clocks do not fight. With push-pull outputs, one master driving high while another drives low would short the supply to ground through the two transistors. With open-drain outputs, low simply wins. SDA works the same way, which is what made the arbitration on slide 52 possible.

A common mistake is to configure I2C pins as push-pull GPIO, or to forget the pull-up resistors. Without pull-ups the line never rises.

Quick check: if one master releases SCL but a slow slave is still holding it low, what voltage does the bus show? The next slides use exactly this behavior to synchronize clocks.

55. Serial Communication – I2C (11/12)

Masters attempt to begin their clock cycles by pulling the SCL line LOW.

Whichever Master pulls the line LOW first instantly forces the entire physical bus to 0V.

The other Masters, seeing the line go LOW, immediately synchronize its internal state machine to this falling edge and beings counting its own programmed LOW period

The synchronized LOW period is strictly determined by the Master with the longest programmed LOW period.

The synchronized HIGH period is strictly determined by the Master with the shortest programmed HIGH period.

If a device pulls down the clock line for a longer time, the result is that all clock generators must enter the wait state. In this way, a slave slows down a fast master, and the slow device creates enough time to store a received byte or to prepare a byte to be transmitted.

Lecture notes

This slide is all text, with no figures. It walks through how two or more I2C masters end up sharing a single clock on the SCL line, building directly on the wired-AND behavior from the previous slide.

Here is the sequence the slide describes. Each master starts a clock cycle by pulling SCL LOW. Whichever master does this first drives the whole bus to 0 V at once, because any single device pulling low wins. The other masters see that falling edge, reset their internal clock state machines to it, and start counting their own programmed LOW periods.

Two rules fall out of the wired-AND, and the slide puts them in bold. The synchronized LOW period is set by the master with the longest programmed LOW time. A master that finishes early releases SCL, but the line stays low while anyone is still holding it, so the early master waits. The synchronized HIGH period is set by the master with the shortest programmed HIGH time. As soon as any master's HIGH count ends, it pulls the line low again, and that ends the HIGH phase for everyone.

The last point applies the same mechanism to slaves, and it is called clock stretching. A slow slave can hold SCL low after a bit, forcing every clock generator into a wait state. This gives the slave time to store a received byte or prepare the next byte to send. A common mistake is assuming the master alone sets the bit rate; on a real bus, the slowest device has the final say.

Quick check: if master A wants 5 microseconds low and 4 high, and master B wants 3 low and 6 high, the shared clock is 5 microseconds low and 4 high.

A common mistake is assuming the master's programmed clock is exactly what appears on the wire. It is only a request; the bus decides.

58. Serial Communication – I2C - Init (1/2)

void I2CA_Init(void) {

// 1. Set the Prescaler to generate the Module Clock

I2caRegs.I2CPSC.all = 16; // Divides high-speed system clock down to ~7-12 MHz

// 2. Define the exact characteristics of the SCL wave

I2caRegs.I2CCLKL = 10; // Defines the duration of the SCL LOW period

I2caRegs.I2CCLKH = 5; // Defines the duration of the SCL HIGH period

// 3. Enable Interrupts for critical network events

I2caRegs.I2CIER.all = 0x24; // Enable Start Condition Detected (SCD) & Register Access Ready (ARDY)

// 4. Take the I2C module out of reset

I2caRegs.I2CMDR.all = 0x0020;

// 5. Enable the 16-level Transmit and Receive FIFOs

I2caRegs.I2CFFTX.all = 0x6000; // Enable FIFO mode and TXFIFO

I2caRegs.I2CFFRX.all = 0x2040; // Enable RXFIFO and clear interrupt flags

}

Notice that I2CCLKL (10) is mathematically larger than I2CCLKH (5). Because the physical bus relies on passive pull-up resistors to transition HIGH, the rise-time is inherently slower, compared to the transistors, than the fall-time (which is actively pulled LOW by a transistor).

Lecture notes

This slide shows the complete initialization function for the I2C-A module on the F28379D, written in C as void I2CA_Init(void). It has five numbered steps, each one or two register writes with a comment.

Step 1 sets the prescaler: I2CPSC = 16. The module clock equals SYSCLK / (I2CPSC + 1). With the 200 MHz system clock that is 200 / 17, about 11.8 MHz, inside the 7 to 12 MHz range the I2C module requires.

Step 2 shapes the SCL clock: I2CCLKL = 10 sets the length of the LOW period and I2CCLKH = 5 sets the HIGH period, both in module-clock cycles. The hardware adds a small fixed offset to each, so together they give an SCL rate of a few hundred kilohertz.

Step 3 writes 0x24 to I2CIER, which enables two interrupts: Start Condition Detected (SCD) and Register Access Ready (ARDY).

Step 4 writes 0x0020 to I2CMDR. That sets the IRS bit, which takes the module out of reset.

Step 5 enables the 16-level FIFOs. I2CFFTX = 0x6000 turns on FIFO mode and the transmit FIFO. I2CFFRX = 0x2040 enables the receive FIFO and clears its interrupt flag.

The note under the code explains why LOW is longer than HIGH. SCL is pulled low actively by a transistor, so it falls fast. It is pulled high only by a passive pull-up resistor charging the bus capacitance, so it rises slowly. The I2C specification also requires a longer minimum low time than high time. This is the same open-drain, wired-AND bus behind the clock synchronization on slide 56.

A common mistake is to change the prescaler or clock registers while the module is running. Notice that this code configures them before setting IRS.

59. Serial Communication – I2C - Init (2/2)

Uint16 I2CA_WriteData(struct I2CMSG *msg) {

// 1. Verify if the previous message completed?

if(I2caRegs.I2CMDR.bit.STP == 1) {

return I2C_STP_NOT_READY_ERROR;

}

// 2. Verify if another Master using the bus?

if(I2caRegs.I2CSTR.bit.BB == 1) {

return I2C_BUS_BUSY_ERROR;

}

// 3. Broadcast the Slave's address (EEPROM is typically 0x50)

I2caRegs.I2CSAR.all = msg->SlaveAddress;

// 4. Define exactly how many bytes we will transmit

// (2 bytes for the EEPROM memory address + the actual data bytes)

I2caRegs.I2CCNT = 2 + msg->NumOfBytes;

// 4. Push the EEPROM memory pointer into the Transmit FIFO (think of the Slave's address as the apartment address and this as setting the specific apartment number we want to write to)

I2caRegs.I2CDXR.all = msg->MemoryHighAddr;

I2caRegs.I2CDXR.all = msg->MemoryLowAddr;

// 5. Push the actual data payload into the Transmit FIFO

for (int i = 0; i < msg->NumOfBytes; i++) {

I2caRegs.I2CDXR.all = *(msg->MsgBuffer + i);

}

// 6. Command the hardware to execute the transaction

// 0x6E20 sets: FREE (run during debug), START, STOP, MASTER, TRANSMIT, and ENABLE bits

I2caRegs.I2CMDR.all = 0x6E20;

return I2C_SUCCESS;

}

Lecture notes

This slide follows the initialization routine from the previous slide with the function that actually sends data: I2CA_WriteData, which writes bytes to an I2C EEPROM through the I2C-A module of the F28379D. It takes a pointer to an I2CMSG structure holding the slave address, the memory address and the data buffer, and it returns a status code.

The code performs six steps. First, it checks the STP bit of the mode register I2CMDR. STP stays 1 until the previous stop condition has gone out, so if it is still set, the function returns I2C_STP_NOT_READY_ERROR. Second, it checks the bus-busy bit BB in the status register I2CSTR. If another master is holding the bus, it returns I2C_BUS_BUSY_ERROR. This is the multi-master problem from a few slides ago. Third, it loads the slave address register I2CSAR. A typical EEPROM sits at 0x50. The address is not sent yet; the hardware sends it automatically after the START condition. Fourth, it sets the count register I2CCNT to 2 + NumOfBytes, because two bytes of EEPROM memory address precede the data. Next, it pushes the memory high and low address bytes into the transmit FIFO through I2CDXR. In the slide's analogy, the slave address is the building, and this memory address is the apartment number. Fifth, a for loop pushes each data byte from MsgBuffer into the FIFO. Sixth, it writes 0x6E20 to I2CMDR. That value sets FREE (0x4000), START (0x2000), STOP (0x0800), MASTER (0x0400), TRANSMIT (0x0200) and the reset-release bit IRS (0x0020). The hardware then runs the whole transaction on its own.

A common mistake: the FIFO is only 16 levels deep, so 2 + NumOfBytes must not exceed 16. That leaves at most 14 data bytes per call.
