The heading reads "Review – 5-minute partner discussion," and the slide asks you to talk with the person next to you about two questions. First: with the IMU onboard the lab robot, how would you measure its current position? Second, an open-ended question: with what you know about the robot and its current sensors, could you send it from point A to point B in a real-world setting? If not, what is missing from the robot? If yes, what assumptions have you made?

For the first question, remember what an IMU measures. The accelerometer gives linear acceleration and the gyro gives angular rate, so neither measures position directly. You would integrate the gyro rate to get heading, theta, then use theta to rotate the body-frame acceleration into the world frame. Integrating that acceleration once gives velocity, and integrating again gives position, for example x_(t+1) = x_t + v_t*dt. The catch is drift. A small constant accelerometer bias b produces a position error of roughly (1/2)*b*t^2, so the error grows with the square of time. An IMU alone is only useful for short intervals.

For the second question, consider what you already have: wheel encoders for dead reckoning and the IMU for heading. Common answers for what is missing are an absolute position reference, a map, and a way to sense obstacles and walls. Hidden assumptions include a flat floor, no wheel slip, a known starting pose, and a clear path.

This slide, titled "Sensors," opens the new topic with a short prompt-and-answer sequence. It states that so far you have explored a couple of ways to sense the robot's internal state: the IMU and the wheel encoders. It then asks, "What are we missing?" and answers, "Sensing external objects!" Finally it asks for examples and lists walls, people, water, doors, and "etc."

The key distinction is between internal and external sensing. Sensors that measure the robot's own state, such as wheel angle from the encoders or angular rate and acceleration from the IMU, are often called proprioceptive. Sensors that measure the world around the robot are called exteroceptive. Encoders and the IMU can estimate how far and in what direction the robot has moved, but they cannot tell it that a wall is half a meter ahead or that a person just stepped into its path. Estimates built only from internal sensors also accumulate drift over time, because there is no outside reference to correct them.

Before the answer appears, pause and ask the class for their own examples. Think about what each would require: detecting a wall needs a distance measurement, detecting water might need a moisture or optical sensor, and recognizing a door or a person may need a camera.

This motivates the rest of the lecture: IR distance sensors and LiDAR, which the robot uses for wall following. Next, sensors are sorted into two categories, passive and active.

Examples:

  • LiDAR
  • Radar
  • Sonar
Lecture notes

This slide divides sensors into two categories. It is text only, with the words "Passive" and "Active" in bold. Passive sensors measure the environment without emitting their own signals; the examples listed are a standard camera and a thermometer. Active sensors emit energy into the environment and measure the reflection or reaction to determine object properties; the examples listed are LiDAR, radar, and sonar.

On the last slide you saw that the IMU and encoders only tell the robot about its own state, and that you also need to sense external things like walls, people, and doors. This slide gives you a first way to classify the sensors that can do that.

The key question is where the energy you measure comes from. A camera records light that is already in the scene, from the sun or room lights, and a thermometer responds to heat that is already present. Neither sends anything out. A LiDAR fires laser pulses, a radar sends radio waves, and a sonar emits sound pulses. Each then times or analyzes the echo to find the distance to an object, and sometimes its speed.

This distinction matters in practice. Passive sensors depend on the environment providing a usable signal, so a camera struggles in the dark. Active sensors bring their own signal, so they work without external light, but they consume more power and can interfere with each other.

Later in this lecture you meet two active sensors in detail, the IR distance sensor and the LiDAR on your robot. Next, try classifying the IMU sensors you already know as passive or active.

Lecture notes

This slide is a quick quiz that applies the passive versus active definitions from the previous slide to the sensors inside the IMU you have already used on the robot. It shows three questions, each followed by its answer in bold. Is the accelerometer passive or active? Passive. Is the gyroscope passive or active? Active, because the gyro has a drive motor, so you have provided energy. Is the magnetometer passive or active? Passive.

Before revealing each answer, pause and let the class vote. The test is whether the sensor puts its own energy into what it measures, or only responds to something that already exists.

The accelerometer is passive. Inside the MEMS chip, a tiny proof mass on springs deflects when the robot accelerates or tilts relative to gravity. The chip reads that deflection, usually as a change in capacitance, without driving the mass.

The magnetometer is also passive. It responds to the Earth's magnetic field and any nearby fields, such as those from motors, and it creates no field of its own.

The gyroscope is the surprise. A MEMS gyro keeps its proof mass constantly vibrating with a built-in drive. When the robot rotates, the Coriolis effect pushes that moving mass sideways, and the size of that sideways motion gives the angular rate. Because the sensor supplies energy to create the motion it measures, the slide classifies it as active.

A common mistake is to call every sensor active because it needs a supply voltage. Every chip needs power to run its electronics. What matters is whether the sensor injects energy in order to make the measurement.

This slide continues the passive versus active quiz from the previous slide, now with application scenarios. It shows three questions in black text, each followed by a centered bold answer. First: which type of sensor works better in complete pitch-black darkness for finding walls? Answer: Active. Second: which type works best for measuring the location of forest fires? Answer: Passive. Third: which type works best for measuring the current environment in the visible spectrum? Answer: Passive, with the note "this is just a camera!"

The rule behind all three is to ask where the energy comes from. In pitch-black darkness there is no ambient light to reflect off the walls, so a passive camera sees nothing. An active sensor such as LiDAR, sonar or an IR rangefinder supplies its own energy and measures the return, so it works regardless of lighting.

A forest fire is itself a strong source of energy, emitting heat as infrared radiation. A passive thermal camera, for example on a satellite or aircraft, simply detects that emission without sending anything out.

For the visible spectrum, sunlight or room lighting already illuminates the scene, so an ordinary camera only needs to collect that light.

A common mistake is to assume that anything "infrared" is active. A thermal camera is passive because it only receives emitted heat. The IR distance sensor on the next slides is active because it carries its own IR LED.

The intensity of the returning light indicates the distance of an object.

  • PSD (position sensitive detector)
  • IR-LED (infrared emitting diode)
Lecture notes

This slide introduces the infrared, or IR, distance sensor. It is all text on a white background. It states the goal: you want to detect whether there is an obstacle in front of the robot, and the simplest way is an active sensor. It then explains that an IR sensor emits an invisible infrared beam and measures the reflection that bounces back, and that the strength of the returning light indicates how far away the object is. Finally, it lists the two parts: a PSD, or position sensitive detector, and an IR-LED, an infrared emitting diode.

This follows from the quiz on the last slide. Finding walls in total darkness calls for an active sensor, one that supplies its own energy instead of relying on ambient light. The IR-LED is that energy source. The PSD is the receiver. It is a light-sensitive strip that reports not only how much light arrives but also where on the strip the reflected spot lands.

Here is the intuitive picture: a near wall sends back a strong reflection, and a far wall sends back a weak one. Keep the word "position" in mind, though. In two slides you see that sensors like the Sharp module use the spot's position on the PSD, a triangulation method, to estimate distance. That makes them less fooled by surface color than pure intensity would be.

Lecture notes

This slide summarizes the advantages and limitations of the IR distance sensor, with a diagram of how it measures distance.

The advantages listed are: simple and inexpensive, small and lightweight, fast response time, and low power consumption. The limitations are: performance depends on surface color and reflectivity; detection range is limited because signal power disperses at a rate of d squared (d^2); the sensor is sensitive to ambient IR light such as sunlight; and it gives a single point of data, like a flashlight beam rather than a wide field of view. The diagram on the right shows the sensor from the side. The IR LED sits under one lens and shines a beam straight up. Two horizontal dashed lines mark a near object and a far object. Light scatters off the object and returns at an angle through a second lens onto a position-sensitive IR detector. A near object returns light at a steeper angle than a far object, so the light spot lands at a different place on the detector. The position of the spot therefore encodes distance. This is called triangulation, and it is why the PSD from the earlier slide is a strip rather than a single photodiode.

A dark, matte surface returns much less light than a white wall, and sunlight floods the detector with IR. In the lab, test your readings against the real surfaces your robot sees. Also remember that one sensor sees only one spot.

Lecture notes

This slide answers a question left over from the limitations list: why do IR distance sensors work poorly at long range, and why would you need a more powerful LED to see farther? The slide gives a one-phrase answer, "Signal dissipation!", followed by three lines of reasoning and one equation.

The reasoning goes like this. When an IR LED emits light, the energy spreads out in all directions. As distance increases, that same energy covers a larger surface area. Therefore the signal intensity, meaning power per unit area, decreases with distance. The slide names this the Inverse Square Law and shows it as I ∝ 1/r^2, read "I is proportional to one over r squared." Here I is the signal intensity and r is the distance the light has traveled from the source.

The square comes from geometry. Light leaving a point source spreads over a sphere whose area is 4*pi*r^2. A real LED emits into a cone rather than a full sphere, but the illuminated area still grows with r^2. So if you double the distance, the intensity drops to one quarter. Triple it, and the intensity drops to one ninth. This is the "disperses at a rate of d^2" limitation from the previous slide, and it is why the Sharp sensor on your robot is only useful over a short window like 4 to 30 cm.

Quick check: if your sensor sees intensity I at 10 cm, what does the object receive at 20 cm? (I/4.)

This slide shows why reflected-light ranging is even harder than the inverse square law from the last slide suggests. On the left, the text says IR distance sensing is worse than 1 over r squared, because the signal travels two paths: emitter to object, then object back to receiver. The returned signal is therefore roughly: received signal proportional to 1 over r to the fourth, written 1/r^4. The slide concludes that seeing far, and rising above noise, takes an extremely powerful LED, and that the same holds for most active sensors such as radar, sonar and LiDAR.

On the right is a NASA JPL Education illustration of the inverse square law. A source of power P radiates through a cone. On a sphere of radius r, the area is 4*pi*r^2, so the intensity is I = P/(4*pi*r^2). A patch of area A at distance r covers 4 patches at 2r and 9 patches at 3r, so the intensity falls to I/4 and then I/9. The source link is jpl.nasa.gov/edu.

Here is why the exponent is 4. The outgoing light spreads as 1/r^2 on its way to the object. The object then scatters what it receives back out in many directions, so the return trip spreads again as 1/r^2. Multiplying the two gives 1/r^4. Doubling the distance cuts the received signal to 1/16, not 1/4.

You can use the typical 2 to 80 cm range from earlier to see the effect. Going from 10 cm to 80 cm is a factor of 8 in distance, so the return falls by 8^4, which is 4096. Surface color and reflectivity, mentioned two slides ago, scale this weak return further.

A common mistake is to assume that doubling LED power doubles the range. Range grows only with the fourth root of power. Quick check: how much more power do you need to double the range? The answer is 16 times.

17. LiDAR (2/12)

What if you want to see the distance of all the obstacles around you instantaneously? Would IR be enough? How would you use IR sensors to create a 360 view of the world?

A basic IR distance sensor only measures distance in one direction.

If we want a complete view of obstacles around a robot, we will need:

  • Many IR sensors
  • Each pointing in a different direction to cover the 360 degrees around the robot

Problems with this approach:

  • Large number of sensors required
  • Limited accuracy (bleed from the other sensors)
  • Slow updates if scanned sequentially
  • Difficult calibration
Lecture notes

This slide is text only, under the title LiDAR. It opens with a question: what if you want to know the distance to every obstacle around you at once? Would IR be enough, and how would you use IR sensors to build a 360-degree view of the world? It then states that a basic IR distance sensor measures distance in only one direction. A full view would need many IR sensors, each pointing in a different direction, to cover all 360 degrees around the robot. It lists four problems with that approach: a large number of sensors, limited accuracy from bleed between sensors, slow updates if the sensors are scanned sequentially, and difficult calibration.

Take a moment with the opening question before going further. Each IR sensor gives one number, the distance along its own beam. To get a ring of measurements, you need one sensor per direction. Spacing them every 10 degrees, for example, already means 36 sensors, each with its own wiring and ADC channel. Bleed, also called crosstalk, happens when one sensor's emitted light reflects into a neighbor's receiver and corrupts that reading. To avoid crosstalk, you can fire the sensors one at a time. That is sequential scanning, and the full ring then refreshes much more slowly. Every sensor also has its own nonlinear voltage-to-distance curve, so calibrating dozens of them is tedious. The previous slide's inverse-square result adds to this: each emitter must be strong to see far.

Quick check: why does firing all the sensors simultaneously make accuracy worse?

18. LiDAR (3/12)

When a single-point IR isn’t enough, we turn to Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR)!

LiDAR solves this by using a laser that scans the environment.

Key idea:

  • Send a laser pulse
  • Measure how long it takes to bounce back
  • Convert that time into distance

Main components:

  • Laser emitter
  • Photodetector
  • Precise timing electronics
  • Rotating motor / scanning system

The system rapidly scans the environment and generates a point cloud, which represents the surrounding world.

Lecture notes

This slide is text only, with no figures. It introduces Light Detection and Ranging, or LiDAR, as the answer to the problem from the previous slide. A single-point IR sensor sees in only one direction, and ringing the robot with many IR sensors means lots of hardware, crosstalk between sensors, slow updates and hard calibration. LiDAR instead uses one laser that scans the environment.

The slide gives the key idea in three steps. First, send a laser pulse. Second, measure how long it takes to bounce back. Third, convert that time into distance. This is different from the IR sensors you just saw, which estimate distance from the strength or angle of the reflected light. LiDAR measures time, which can be done very precisely.

The slide lists four main components. The laser emitter produces the short light pulse. The photodetector senses the reflected light when it returns. The precise timing electronics measure the travel time; light covers about 30 centimeters per nanosecond, so the timing must be extremely fine. The rotating motor or scanning system sweeps the beam around the robot so that one rangefinder covers all directions.

The result is a point cloud, the term in bold on the slide. A point cloud is a large set of measured points that represents the surrounding world. For a 2D spinning LiDAR, each reading is an angle theta and a range r. You can convert it to a point with x = r*cos(theta) and y = r*sin(theta).

This slide is text only. It gives two reasons to use a state machine and then describes the two states for right-wall following, each with the condition that makes the robot leave it.

The first reason is behavioral. Separating tasks lets the robot carry out complex behaviors that are independent of each other, because each state does one job well. The second reason is software. Each state can live in its own function or file instead of one large function full of nested if statements. That makes your code easier to debug, since you know which state was active when something went wrong, and easier to extend when you want to add a new state later.

For right-wall following there are two states. State 1 holds the robot at a specific distance from the right wall, using the LiDAR readings. If the robot sees an obstacle in front, such as another wall, it switches to State 2. State 2 handles that obstacle: to avoid a collision, the robot stops and turns left. Once it no longer sees an obstacle ahead, it returns to State 1 and continues following the wall on its right.

A common mistake is to mix the transition checks into the control math so the logic becomes hard to follow. Keep the two parts separate: each state computes its outputs, then checks its exit condition.

A critical flaw in naive FSM design is "chattering" (Zeno behavior), where minor sensor noise causes the logic to rapidly oscillate between States 1 and State 2 infinitely fast.

To prevent this, we ensure a robust enough “deadband / hysteresis” zone into the transition boundaries.

A deadband zone typically represents an inactive region where no action is taken.

For example, instead of setting a velocity of close to zero, just set it to zero to reduce power consumption and potential small noise or backlash. When |v|<ε|v|\ < \varepsilon, set v=0v\ = \ 0.

Lecture notes

This slide names the main weakness of the two-state wall-following machine you just saw: chattering, also called Zeno behavior. Zeno behavior means the system makes an infinite number of jumps in finite time. On the robot, if the front LiDAR distance hovers right at a single transition threshold, small sensor noise pushes it back and forth across that line, and the logic flips between State 1 (right wall follow) and State 2 (left turn) on nearly every sample. The motors receive alternating commands and the robot jitters instead of committing to either behavior.

The fix the slide proposes is to build a deadband, or hysteresis, zone into the transition boundaries. A deadband is an inactive region where no action is taken. The slide's example applies this to a velocity command: rather than sending a velocity that is almost zero, set it exactly to zero. In words, when the absolute value of v is less than epsilon, set v to zero, written as if |v| < epsilon then v = 0. This saves power and avoids reacting to small noise or gear backlash.

The plot on the right shows a deadband with both axes running from minus 1 to 1. The input is a solid blue diagonal line, where output equals input. The dashed red output lies on that line except between minus 0.5 and 0.5, where it is held at zero. At plus and minus 0.5 it jumps vertically back onto the line. So in this plot epsilon is 0.5. Any input smaller than 0.5 in magnitude produces no output, and larger inputs pass through unchanged.

A common mistake is choosing an epsilon smaller than your sensor's noise level. If you do that, the deadband does nothing to stop the chatter.

This slide defines hysteresis, the second tool against chattering after the deadband on the previous slide. The text reads: hysteresis is path-dependent, where the output relies on whether the variable is rising or falling.

The figure plots output on the vertical axis against input on the horizontal axis. A dashed orange diagonal line marks the ideal one-to-one response, where each input always gives the same output. Around it, two green curves form a lens-shaped loop. Both curves start at the origin and meet again at the upper right. When the input increases, the output follows the lower curve, below the diagonal; the arrows on that curve point up and to the right. When the input decreases, the output comes back along the upper curve, above the diagonal, with arrows pointing down and to the left. A dashed vertical line at one input value crosses both curves. The double-headed arrow between those two crossings is labeled "Hysteresis": at the same input there are two possible outputs.

The key idea is memory. With hysteresis, you cannot predict the output from the current input alone. You also need to know which direction the input came from, which means knowing the current state. A home thermostat works this way. It turns the heat on at 19 degrees and off at 21, so it does not click on and off around 20.

For the wall follower, the same idea means using two different thresholds instead of one. One threshold switches the robot into the turn. A different threshold switches it back to wall following. Sensor noise near either threshold can then no longer flip the state back and forth.

44. Wall Following – State Machine (7/7)

Lecture notes

This slide revisits the wall-following state machine, extended into a full micromouse controller like the one in the video. The diagram shows nine states as circles connected by labeled transition arrows.

At power on, the robot enters State 0, Idle/Calibrate: v = 0 and omega = 0, the IMU is zeroed and SLAM is initialized. A pushbutton press, with the IMU ready flag equal to 1, moves it to State 1, Search Forward. Here v = v_search and omega = K_p*(d_ref - d_side), which is "omega equals K p times the reference wall distance minus the measured side distance." This is the wall-following controller from earlier in the lecture.

When the encoder distance reaches one cell length, L_cell, the robot enters State 4, SLAM and Flood-Fill. It updates the occupancy grid, recalculates the path cost, and then branches:

1. If the optimal path is forward, it returns to State 1.

2. If the path is left or right, it goes to State 2, a 90 degree pivot with v = 0 and omega = K_t*(theta_target - theta_gyro). It returns to State 1 when the heading error is about zero.

3. If three walls are closed (a dead end), it goes to State 3, a 180 degree U-turn at saturated omega_max. It returns to State 1 once it has turned 180 degrees.

4. If the center coordinate is reached, it goes to State 5, Goal Reached, which optimizes a diagonal path and flashes a ready LED.

A second button press starts State 6, Speed Sprint, at v_max with gyro heading hold. When d_error < d_brake near a waypoint, the robot switches to State 7, High-G Curve. The vacuum fan turns on and the robot tracks a clothoid trajectory, then returns to State 6 when the curve exit vector is achieved. The finish line, or any collision or fault, triggers State 8, Hard Deceleration: a = -a_max until v reaches zero, then back to State 0.

One coding tip: never test floats with ==. Write encoder distance >= L_cell.
