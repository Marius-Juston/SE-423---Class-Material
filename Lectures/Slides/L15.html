Take stock of what you already have. Obstacle-sensing sensors, such as the distance sensors and LiDAR, tell the robot what is around it. Internal sensors, such as the wheel encoders and the gyro, tell it how its wheels are turning and how it is rotating. Your wall-following and drive-straight controllers work in relative terms. They hold a distance or a heading compared with something nearby, and they never need to know where on the map the robot is.

A goal like (5, 5) is different. It is an absolute position in a map frame. To steer toward it, the robot must know its own pose: its x position, its y position, and its heading angle theta, all in that same frame. Localization is the problem of estimating that pose.

Quick check: could your wall-following code alone get the robot to (5, 5)? Why not?

7. Localization (2/5)

To solve localization, we need to understand that the world is not perfect and not a single sensor gives the exact solution.

We first estimate movement using internal sensors (Dead-Reckoning).

What is a problem with just using internal sensors?

The internal sensors drift and accumulate errors!

Think of this as walking blind and relying on just counting your steps as you did at the beginning of the lecture.

Finally, we use external sensors (LiDAR, Cameras) to look at landmarks and correct these errors (SLAM)

Lecture notes

This slide is text only, under the title Localization. It lays out the overall strategy in steps. First, no single sensor gives the exact answer, because the world is not perfect. Second, you estimate movement with internal sensors, which is called dead-reckoning. Third, the slide asks what goes wrong with using only internal sensors, and answers that they drift and accumulate errors. It compares this to walking blind and counting your steps, as you did at the beginning of the lecture. Finally, external sensors such as LiDAR and cameras look at landmarks to correct those errors, which leads to SLAM.

The key idea is that localization combines two kinds of information. Internal sensors measure the robot's own motion. On our robot these are the wheel encoders, and possibly a gyro. Dead-reckoning integrates those measurements over time: add up how far each wheel turned and you get a position estimate.

The catch is that every small error gets added in too. Wheel slip, a slightly wrong wheel radius or a small gyro bias each contribute, and because the estimate is a running sum, the errors never cancel out. They grow the longer you drive.

External sensors fix this by measuring something fixed in the world, such as a wall or a landmark, and pulling the estimate back toward the truth. SLAM, simultaneous localization and mapping, does this while also building the map.

Quick check: if your step count were off by just 2 percent, would your position error after 100 steps be larger or smaller than after 10 steps?

Before you can do any of this, you need a way to describe positions in different frames, so next we look at coordinate transformations.

9. Localization (3/5)

A robot experiences the world from its own perspective, which we call the Local Frame (or Body Frame).

The map or the room exists in a fixed, global reference frame, called the Global / World Frame (or Inertial Frame).

When driving a car, your local reference frame is your point of view inside the car, only you have that viewpoint, and the global reference is the shared viewpoint, which is provided by the GPS map.

Each of the sensors view its information in its own Local Frame; however, to navigate a room, we must translate the local sensor data into the global map!

Lecture notes

This slide is all text, with no figures. It defines the two reference frames used throughout the rest of the lecture. Key terms appear in bold: Local Frame and Global / World Frame.

First, a robot experiences the world from its own perspective, called the Local Frame or Body Frame. The frame is attached to the robot and moves and turns with it. When a sensor reports "an obstacle 2 meters ahead," that means ahead of the robot, wherever the robot happens to be pointing.

Second, the map or the room exists in a fixed global reference frame, called the Global, World, or Inertial Frame. Its origin and axes never move, so every location in the room has one agreed-upon set of coordinates.

The car analogy ties these together. Your local frame is your point of view inside the car, and only you have it. "The exit is on my right" means nothing to someone else unless they know where you are and which way you face. The GPS map is the shared global viewpoint that everyone can use.

The last point is the reason this matters. Every sensor reports data in its own local frame, so the robot has to translate that data into the global map before it can navigate a room.

A quick check for you: if the robot turns 90 degrees in place, do its local coordinates of a wall change? Do the wall's world coordinates change? The local coordinates change, but the world coordinates do not.

Next, you see that each sensor can have its own frame, offset and rotated from the robot's body frame. That leads to a chain of transforms: sensor frame to body frame, then body frame to world frame.

If the robot’s heading θ=0\theta = \ 0, its axes are perfectly aligned with the world axes.

Thus, moving from the robot frame to the world frame is simply a vector addition!

If the robot is at world coordinates T=(x,y)T\ = \ (x,y), a point PR=(xr,yr)P_{R} = \left( x_{r},y_{r} \right) in the robot frame is:

Pw=(xr+x,yr+y)P_{w} = \left( x_{r} + x,y_{r} + y \right)

PW=PR+TP_{W} = P_{R} + T

Lecture notes

On the last slide you defined the world frame, the body frame, and the robot's pose: its position plus its heading angle. Now you start building the transform between those frames, beginning with the simplest case.

The slide is all text and equations. It says that if the robot's heading theta = 0, its axes are perfectly aligned with the world axes. Moving from the robot frame to the world frame is then just vector addition. 14. Localization – 2D Rotation (1/4)

Suppose the robot frame is at the world origin but rotated by and angle φ\varphi.

We want to map point PP, from the rotated frame to the fixed frame.

Let point PP have polar coordinates (r,α)(r,\alpha)

Therefore,

(xr,yr)=(rcos(α),rsin(α))\left( x_{r},y_{r} \right) = \left( r\cos(\alpha),r\sin(\alpha) \right)

Lecture notes

This slide starts the second piece of a coordinate transform: rotation. The text reads: suppose the robot frame is at the world origin but rotated by an angle phi. We want to map a point P from the rotated frame to the fixed frame. Let point P have polar coordinates (r, alpha). Therefore, centered on the slide: (x_r, y_r) = (r*cos(alpha), r*sin(alpha)), read as "x sub r, y sub r equals r cosine alpha, r sine alpha."

On the last two slides you handled pure translation, where the robot's axes stay parallel to the world axes and converting a point is just vector addition. Now we isolate the other case. The two frames share an origin, so there is no shift, only a turn by phi.

The trick is to describe P in polar form. Here r is the distance from the shared origin to P, and alpha is the angle of P measured from the robot's own X_B axis. The subscript r means these are robot-frame coordinates. Converting polar to Cartesian in the robot frame gives x_r = r*cos(alpha) and y_r = r*sin(alpha). Nothing about the world frame has entered yet. This is just the point as the robot sees it.

A common mistake is to treat alpha as a world angle. It is not. Alpha is relative to the robot's heading. The distance r is the same in both frames, because a rotation does not change lengths.

This is exactly the form your LiDAR data arrives in: each return is a range r and a bearing alpha relative to the robot. After the next slide's diagram, slide 16 shows that in the world frame the angle becomes alpha + phi, and then expands it with the trigonometric sum identities.

16. Localization – 2D Rotation (2/4)

In the world frame, the angle of point PP is now at (α+φ)(\alpha + \varphi).

The world coordinates are thus,

Pw=(rcos(α+φ),rsin(α+φ))P_{w} = \left( r\cos(\alpha + \varphi),r\sin(\alpha + \varphi) \right)

We can expand the following using standard trigonometric sum identities:

xW=r(cos(α)cos(φ)−sin(α)sin(φ))x_{W} = r\left( \cos(\alpha)\cos(\varphi) - \sin(\alpha)\sin(\varphi) \right)

yW=r(sin(α)cos(φ)+cos(α)sin(φ))y_{W} = r\left( \sin(\alpha)\cos(\varphi) + \cos(\alpha)\sin(\varphi) \right)

Lecture notes

This slide finishes the geometric argument for rotation. Picking up from the setup on slide 14, point P has polar coordinates (r, alpha) in the robot's body frame: it sits a distance r from the origin at angle alpha measured from the body X axis. The body frame itself is rotated by phi relative to the world frame, and both frames share the same origin.

The first line says that in the world frame, the angle of point P is now alpha + phi. The point has not moved and its distance r from the origin is unchanged. Only the reference direction changed, so the two angles simply add.

The world coordinates are therefore P_W = (r*cos(alpha + phi), r*sin(alpha + phi)), read as "P sub W equals r cosine of alpha plus phi, comma, r sine of alpha plus phi."

Next the slide applies the standard trigonometric sum identities:

x_W = r*(cos(alpha)*cos(phi) - sin(alpha)*sin(phi))

y_W = r*(sin(alpha)*cos(phi) + cos(alpha)*sin(phi))

In words, x sub W equals r times the quantity cosine alpha cosine phi minus sine alpha sine phi. Y sub W equals r times the quantity sine alpha cosine phi plus cosine alpha sine phi.

The point of expanding is to separate alpha, which belongs to the point, from phi, which belongs to the robot's heading. Notice that r*cos(alpha) and r*sin(alpha) are hiding in these expressions. Those are just the body-frame coordinates x_B and y_B. On the next slide you substitute them back, and the result becomes a matrix multiplication.

A common slip is the sign: the minus sign belongs in the cosine identity, not the sine identity. As a quick check, set phi = 0. You should recover x_W = r*cos(alpha) and y_W = r*sin(alpha), so the two frames coincide.

17. Localization – 2D Rotation (3/4)

Substituting back in the location position:

(xr,yr)=(rcos(α),rsin(α))\left( x_{r},y_{r} \right) = \left( r\cos(\alpha),r\sin(\alpha) \right)

To the global position:

xW=r(cos(α)cos(φ)−sin(α)sin(φ))x_{W} = r\left( \cos(\alpha)\cos(\varphi) - \sin(\alpha)\sin(\varphi) \right)

yW=r(sin(α)cos(φ)+cos(α)sin(φ))y_{W} = r\left( \sin(\alpha)\cos(\varphi) + \cos(\alpha)\sin(\varphi) \right)

We thus get:

xW=xRcos⁡(φ)−yRsin⁡(φ)x_{W} = x_{R}\cos(\varphi) - y_{R}\sin(\varphi)

yW=xRsin⁡(φ)+yRcos⁡(φ)y_{W} = x_{R}\sin(\varphi) + y_{R}\cos(\varphi)

Which is elegantly expressed using matrix multiplications!

Lecture notes

This slide finishes the rotation derivation. It shows three groups of equations. First, the point's coordinates in the robot (body) frame: (x_r, y_r) = (r*cos(alpha), r*sin(alpha)), meaning x_r equals r times cosine of alpha and y_r equals r times sine of alpha. Second, the world-frame coordinates after the trig sum identities from the previous slide: x_W = r*(cos(alpha)*cos(phi) - sin(alpha)*sin(phi)) and y_W = r*(sin(alpha)*cos(phi) + cos(alpha)*sin(phi)). Third, the result: x_W = x_R*cos(phi) - y_R*sin(phi) and y_W = x_R*sin(phi) + y_R*cos(phi). The slide closes with "Which is elegantly expressed using matrix multiplications!"

The key step is substitution. On the last slide you wrote the world position as r*cos(alpha + phi) and r*sin(alpha + phi) and expanded those. Distribute the r into each term and you find r*cos(alpha) and r*sin(alpha), which are exactly x_R and y_R. So r and alpha drop out. You no longer need the point's distance or angle, only its body-frame coordinates and the robot's heading phi. That is what makes this practical on the robot: a sensor such as the LiDAR reports points relative to the robot, and these two lines place them on the world map.

We assume the noise is "white noise." Its distribution is written epsilon_noise ~ N(0, sigma^2), which reads "normally distributed with mean zero and variance sigma squared." Zero mean means it averages out on any single reading. The bias does not average out.

To get heading, you integrate the rate, and the bias gets integrated right along with it. The slide writes this as the integral of epsilon_bias dt = epsilon_bias * Delta t. A constant integrated over time grows linearly with elapsed time.

Here is the example the slide works. The bias is only 0.05 degrees per second, and you drive for 5 minutes, which is 300 seconds. Then 0.05 * 300 = 15 degrees of heading error. That is enough to send your robot into a wall on the far side of the room.

This is why we calibrate the IMU. You hold the robot still, so the true rate is zero. You average many gyro readings, and that average estimates the bias. You then subtract it from every later reading. A common mistake is bumping or moving the robot during this calibration window, which bakes a wrong bias into every measurement after it.

Last slide we chose the Z-axis gyro to fix heading drift from the encoders, and here we remove its bias. Next we see what the leftover white noise does when you integrate it.

Lecture notes

This slide picks up right after bias calibration. Once you measure and subtract the gyro's bias, what remains is white noise. The slide shows the gyro model in words: omega gyro at time t equals omega true at time t plus epsilon noise, written omega_(gyro,t) = omega_(true,t) + epsilon_noise. Here epsilon_noise is zero-mean Gaussian white noise, as defined on the previous slide.

The key idea is what happens when you integrate that noise to get heading. Integrating white noise produces a "random walk," which stochastic calculus calls a Wiener process. Each tiny error is random, but you keep adding them up, so the heading error wanders away from the truth and never resets.

The slide gives three properties. First, if you repeat many runs, the mean of the heading error is centered at 0, so on average there is no preferred direction. Second, the variance of the heading error grows linearly with time: sigma phi squared equals sigma omega squared times delta t, written sigma_phi^2 = sigma_omega^2 * Delta t. Here phi is the integrated angle, sigma_omega describes the gyro noise, and Delta t is the elapsed integration time. Third, the standard deviation, which is the actual typical angular error you see, grows proportional to the square root of time, sqrt(t).

The slide says to take the proof as given, but here is some intuition. When you add independent random errors, their variances add. Doubling the time doubles the variance, so the standard deviation grows only by a factor of sqrt(2).

A common mistake is thinking that "zero mean" means the error cancels out. It does not. Any single run, like your robot's, still drifts, and that drift is unbounded. Over a long period, heading from the gyro alone becomes entirely unreliable.

Sensor fusion is the mathematical process of combining these complementary sensors. We use algorithms such as complementary filters or Kalman filters to merge the data.

The previous two plots showed why the gyro drifts. Heading from a gyro is the integral of measured angular rate, so every small random error gets summed. The spread grows like sigma*sqrt(t), and any bias adds on top of that. So the gyro is trustworthy over seconds but not over minutes.

Encoders fail in a different way. In dead reckoning, heading comes from the difference of the left and right wheel distances divided by the track width. A small slip on one wheel, which is common during fast turns, becomes a permanent heading error. Every later position estimate then inherits that error.

The key word is complementary. The two sensors make errors in different situations and on different time scales, so combining them gives an estimate better than either one alone.

A common mistake is assuming that a gyro with low noise never needs correction. Integration guarantees that its error eventually grows without bound.

This slide gives a brief look at the simplest sensor-fusion method from the previous slide, the complementary filter. The text says it blends a high-pass filtered signal with a low-pass filtered signal. For heading, the integrated gyro data goes through the high-pass path because we trust it in the short term. An absolute heading measurement, such as a magnetometer if one is available, goes through the low-pass path because we trust it in the long term.

The centered equation reads: phi_fused equals alpha times the quantity phi_fused plus omega_gyro times delta t, plus one minus alpha times phi_mag. In plain text: phi_fused = alpha*(phi_fused + omega_gyro*dt) + (1 - alpha)*phi_mag. Alpha is a tuning constant close to 1, for example 0.98. The slide notes that this is essentially an exponential moving average.

Here is how to read it. The first term is a gyro prediction: take the last fused heading and add the rotation measured during this sample. The second term gently pulls that prediction toward the magnetometer. With alpha = 0.98, each update is 98 percent gyro prediction and 2 percent magnetometer. Fast motion therefore comes from the smooth gyro. Slow gyro drift, the square-root-of-time growth you saw two slides ago, is steadily corrected by the absolute sensor. The magnetometer's high-frequency noise is averaged away.

A useful rule of thumb is that the filter's time constant is about tau = alpha*dt/(1 - alpha). For example, with dt = 0.01 s, tau is about 0.49 s.

To check your understanding, ask what alpha = 1 gives. It gives pure gyro integration, which drifts. Alpha = 0 gives the raw, noisy magnetometer.

On the robot, watch for angle wrap-around near plus or minus pi. Also watch for magnetic disturbance from the motors.

This slide is text only, under the title SLAM. It says the two sensors on our robot that can see the outside world are the LiDAR and the camera. With the camera, you can place purpose-made tags in the environment as landmarks, and the most common family is called AprilTags. An AprilTag is a square black-and-white pattern. First, look at each image and find pixels that stand out as landmarks, such as corners or edges. These are called features.

Second, in the next frame, match those features to the ones you found before.

Third, calculate the 6DOF motion of the camera that explains how the matched features moved. 6DOF means six degrees of freedom: three translations (x, y, z) and three rotations (roll, pitch, yaw).

Fourth, chain these frame-to-frame motions together to get odometry.

Fifth, if you return to a place you have seen before, use loop closure to correct the drift that built up along the way.

A common misconception is that visual odometry alone stops drift. It does not, because small errors still accumulate from frame to frame. Only loop closure, or a known landmark, removes them.

Lecture notes

This slide is text only, titled SLAM. It asks: what if there are no AprilTags and we do not know the map beforehand? The answer is SLAM, Simultaneous Localization and Mapping. The spinning LiDAR produces a dense 2D point cloud. As the robot moves, it takes a new scan and compares it to the previous one. To align two point clouds taken a few seconds apart, the Iterative Closest Point algorithm, ICP, rotates and translates the new scan until it best overlaps the old one. That rotation and translation is exactly how much the robot moved, which gives accurate odometry without relying on wheel friction.

On the last slide you saw the camera route: landmarks such as AprilTags, or hard-to-track natural features. The LiDAR route uses the robot's own scans as the landmarks. A point cloud is simply the list of (x, y) points where the laser hit a wall or object.

ICP repeats three steps. First, pair each new point with its closest point in the old scan. Second, solve for the rotation angle theta and translation t that best line up those pairs, so each point becomes p_new = R(theta)*p + t. Third, apply that transform and repeat until the mean error stops shrinking. This is the same 2D rotation and translation from earlier in today's lecture.

Here is why this matters. Wheel dead reckoning drifts whenever the wheels slip, and those are the errors we just studied. Scan matching measures motion against the walls themselves.

A common pitfall is that ICP needs a reasonable initial guess and distinctive geometry. In a long, featureless hallway it can slide. Small errors still accumulate, which is why loop closure matters.

ICP repeats four steps. First, match each source point to its closest target point. Second, find the rotation R and translation t that minimize the sum of squared distances, sum of ||R*a_i + t - b_i||^2. Third, apply that R and t to the source. Fourth, recompute the mean error, and stop when it stops decreasing.

The point-to-point version minimizes straight-line distance between matched points. The point-to-plane version minimizes distance along the target surface normal. That lets points slide along flat surfaces such as walls, and it usually converges in fewer iterations.

A common pitfall is that ICP only finds the nearest good fit. If the initial misalignment is too large, the closest-point matches are wrong and ICP converges to a bad answer. On your robot, dead-reckoning provides the initial guess.
