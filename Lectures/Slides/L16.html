2. Review – 5-minute partner discussion

Talk to the person next to you and answer these questions.

  • Why did we use a ping pong buffer for the FFT code?
  • Given that LiDAR splits its space as 360 degrees into 1024 steps, what is the angular resolution of the LiDAR?
  • What happens if your angular resolution is too low and your object is far away?
  • Is UART a synchronous or asynchronous communication protocol? What does it mean for it to be synchronous or not?
Lecture notes

This slide is a review activity titled "Review – 5-minute partner discussion." It asks you to talk with the person next to you and answer four questions: why we used a ping pong buffer for the FFT code; what the angular resolution of the LiDAR is, given that it splits 360 degrees into 1024 steps; what happens if your angular resolution is too low and the object is far away; and whether UART is synchronous or asynchronous, and what that means.

Take the five minutes with your partner first, then compare with these answers.

Ping pong buffer: the ADC keeps sampling while the FFT runs. With two buffers, new samples fill one buffer while the FFT processes the other, and then the two swap roles. Samples are never lost, and the data being transformed is never overwritten mid-computation.

Angular resolution: 360/1024 = 0.3515625, or about 0.35 degrees per step. In radians that is 2*pi/1024, about 0.00614 rad.

Low resolution at long range: the gap between neighboring beams grows with distance, spacing = r*delta_theta. At 4 m, 4*0.00614 is about 2.5 cm, so a thin object like a chair leg may get only one point or fall between beams entirely. Your map then misses it or misjudges its edges. A common mistake is to multiply by delta_theta in degrees instead of radians.

UART is asynchronous. There is no shared clock wire. Both sides agree in advance on a baud rate, and each byte is framed by start and stop bits so the receiver can resynchronize. A synchronous protocol such as SPI sends a clock line alongside the data.

These ideas return today: the LiDAR streams its scans to the Red Board over a serial link, and we parse them.

This slide has a plain white layout with the title "LiDAR" and two text points, no figures. The first point says the LiDAR you work with in this course is called the URG-04LX. The second says it is a laser sensor for area scanning, and that its light source is an infrared laser with a wavelength of 785 nanometers, rated laser class 1 for safety. The slide text writes the second name as "RG-04LX"; that is a typo, and both points refer to the same URG-04LX.

LiDAR stands for light detection and ranging. The sensor sends out laser pulses, measures how long the light takes to reflect back, and turns that time into a distance. "Area scanning" means the URG-04LX sweeps its beam through an arc in a single plane. Each scan therefore gives you a fan of distance readings at many angles around the robot. That is exactly the data you need later for obstacle avoidance and LiDAR SLAM.

The wavelength matters. Visible light ends at roughly 700 nanometers, so 785 nanometers is near-infrared and invisible to your eye. You cannot tell whether the laser is on just by looking at it. Do not stare into the sensor window to check; read the sensor's status through software instead.

Class 1 is the safest laser classification: the sensor is considered safe under normal use. Next you see what the laser classes mean and what eye, skin and fire hazards the higher classes carry.

This slide continues the eye-hazard discussion from the previous slide and explains why the wavelength of a laser matters so much. On the right is the same cross-section of the human eye. Light from a candle passes through the cornea, pupil and lens, which focus the rays so they cross inside the eye and form an inverted image of the candle on the retina, next to the optic nerve.

Lecture notes

This slide, titled "LiDAR – Laser classes – Skin," is text only, with three points about how lasers can injure skin. It follows the two slides on eye hazards.

The first point uses an analogy. If you had to put either your eyes or your hands on a hot stove, you would choose your hands. Skin hazards are less serious than eye hazards, but skin burns still deserve attention. the laser's output power,

the wavelength,

the size of the affected area,

and the duration of the irradiation, meaning how long the skin is exposed.

As a quick check, ask yourself why a tightly focused beam is worse than a wide one at the same power. The answer is that the energy is concentrated on a smaller area.

Recall the difference between the two kinds of reflection from the skin slide. A specular reflection is mirror-like and keeps the beam concentrated. 14. LiDAR (2/5)

  • Scan area is 240º semicircle with maximum distance of 4000mm.
  • Pitch angle is 0.36º and sensor outputs the distance measured at every point (683 steps).
  • Laser beam diameter is less than 20mm at 2000mm with maximum divergence 40mm at 4000mm.
  • The principle of distance measurement is based on calculation of the phase difference, due to which it is possible to obtain stable measurement with minimum influence from object’s color and surface gloss.
    • Essentially Time of Flight but with a continuous wave rather than the discrete pulse
Lecture notes

This slide gives the key specifications of the Hokuyo URG-04LX, the 2D scanning LiDAR on your robot. It is all text, four bullets plus one sub-bullet.

First, the sensor scans a 240 degree arc, wider than a true semicircle, out to a maximum range of 4000 mm, or 4 meters. The remaining 120 degrees behind the sensor is blind.

Second, the angular step, called the pitch angle, is 0.36 degrees, and the sensor reports one distance at every step, 683 steps in total. If you divide 240 by 0.36 you get about 667, not 683. That is because the exact step is 360/1024, about 0.352 degrees, which the slide rounds to 0.36. Use the exact value when you convert a step index into an angle in your code.

Third, the laser beam is less than 20 mm in diameter at 2000 mm and spreads to at most 40 mm at 4000 mm. The beam's footprint therefore grows with distance, so thin objects far away, such as a chair leg, may give blended or missed readings.

Fourth, distance comes from phase difference. The sensor emits a continuously modulated wave and compares the phase of the returning light with the outgoing light. In words, distance equals the speed of light times the phase shift divided by four pi times the modulation frequency: d = c*delta_phi/(4*pi*f). This is essentially time of flight, but with a continuous wave instead of a discrete pulse. It makes readings stable regardless of an object's color or gloss.

Quick check: roughly how far apart are neighboring readings on a wall 4 m away? About 4000*0.00614 rad, or about 25 mm.

Lecture notes

Before you can send individual commands, you need to know the shape of every message on the serial (SCI) line. This slide gives the general packet format the URG-04LX uses in both directions, and every command on the following slides fills in this template.

The first table, Host to Sensor, has three boxes: Command, then Parameter (if necessary), then a terminator, either LF, hex 0a, or CR, hex 0d. So the Red Board sends a command letter, any parameter characters, and one line-ending character.

The second table, Sensor to Host, has eight boxes: Command, Parameter (if necessary), LF, Status, LF, Data, LF, LF. The sensor first echoes your command and parameter, then sends a status line, then the data, and ends with two line feeds in a row. That double line feed is how your receive code knows the reply is complete.

The bullets explain the notation. The terminator can be Line Feed, written '\n' in C, or Carriage Return, '\r'. A value written with a subscript 16, like 0a_16, is an ASCII code in hexadecimal. Any status other than the character '0' means an error.

A common mistake is comparing the status byte to the number 0. The sensor sends the ASCII character '0', which is 0x30, so compare against '0'. Another is forgetting the terminator: without '\n', the sensor never acts on your command.

Lecture notes

This slide covers the first and simplest of the four LiDAR commands from two slides back: the version command, a single capital V.

On the left are two message-format tables. Host to sensor is just two fields: the character V, which is hex 56, followed by a line feed or carriage return. Sensor to host is a stack of lines, each ending in a line feed: the echoed V, a status code, vendor information, product information, firmware version, protocol version, and sensor serial number. The last line ends with two line feeds, which marks the end of the reply. On the right is an example reply: V; status 0; VEND: Hokuyo Automatic Co., Ltd.; PROD: SOKUIKI Sensor URG-X002; FIRM: 0.01.22a, dated 2004/12/27; PROT: 00001, SCIP 1.0; SERI: H0400000.

Notice the reply follows the general packet format from the previous slide. The sensor echoes your command, then sends a status. Status 0 means success; anything else is an error. The double line feed is how your receive code knows the message is complete.

At the bottom is the code: serial_sendSCIC(&SerialC, "V\n", 2); sends it on SCI C. The 2 is the number of bytes: the V and the newline. A common mistake is to pass a length that leaves off the terminator. Without the line feed or carriage return, the sensor never sees a complete command and does not reply.

Version info is a useful first test in lab. If you get this reply back, your wiring, baud rate, and serial code all work. Next, you use the L command to turn the laser on and off.

Lecture notes

This slide covers the laser illumination command, the "L" command, which turns the URG-04LX's laser on or off. The slide also notes that the laser is on by default whenever the sensor starts up, even if you switched it off before.

Two packet tables show the format. From host to sensor there are three fields: the character L (hex 4C), a one-byte control code, and a line feed or carriage return.   • To represents the data points in ASCII format a simple encoding is implemented.

Example:

1234 mm = 01001101001022

↓ separation

(0100112, 0100102) = (1316, 1216)

↓ Add 3016

To decode you just do the inverse, where you subtract 3016 and then merge using bitwise or and bit shifting. It follows the big-endian system. The most significant bits of the data are sent first (in this case ‘C’ then ‘D’).

Lecture notes

This slide explains how the URG-04LX packs each distance reading into ASCII characters before sending it over the serial port. After the version, laser, and baud-rate commands, this is the format you will decode when you request scans.

The slide makes four points. The sensor measures up to 4095 millimeters with 1 millimeter resolution, so each reading fits in 12 bits (range 0 to 4095). To send it as text, the 12 bits are split into two 6-bit pieces, each with a maximum value of 63, and hexadecimal 30, the character '0', is added to each piece.

The worked example uses 1234 millimeters. In 12-bit binary that is 010011010010. The upper six bits are 010011, which is hex 13 or decimal 19. The lower six bits are 010010, which is hex 12 or decimal 18. Adding hex 30 gives hex 43 and hex 42. Hex 43 is 'C'. The slide labels hex 42 as 'D', but in ASCII hex 42 is actually 'B', and 'D' is hex 44. So 1234 millimeters arrives as "CB".

Why encode it this way? Adding hex 30 keeps every data byte printable, between '0' (hex 30) and 'o' (hex 6F). This means a data byte can never look like the line feed that ends a line in the reply.

To decode, reverse the steps:

distance = ((c1 - 0x30) << 6) | (c2 - 0x30)

Check it with the example: 19 << 6 is 1216, and 1216 + 18 = 1234. The format is big-endian, so the most significant character is sent first.

A common mistake is swapping the two characters, or forgetting to subtract hex 30 before shifting.

This slide shows how the robot decodes the LiDAR's reply to the G command. On the left is the sensor-to-host reply format. The first line echoes the command: the character 'G' (hex 47), a 3-digit starting point, a 3-digit end point, a 2-digit cluster count, then a line feed (LF). Next is a Status line ending in LF. Receiving 'G' sets G_count to 1 and moves to State 1, which counts header bytes and loops while G_count < 11. The 11th byte is the status. A linefeed in either data state does not toggle get_dis_first. It only increments LF_count. When LF_count reaches 2, the stream has ended: the interrupt triggers SWI2 (INTx10), toggles the ping/pong flag, and returns to State 0. This slide converts LiDAR readings into world coordinates, the step left as a placeholder in the SWI2 code on the previous slide. It has two parts: locate the LiDAR in the world, then locate each detected object.

First, the slide shows a homogeneous transformation in matrix form. The vector x_W, y_W, 1 equals a 3 by 3 matrix times the vector x_R, y_R, 1. The matrix's first row is cos(theta), minus sin(theta), t_x; the second row is sin(theta), cos(theta), t_y; the third row is 0, 0, 1. The two code lines implement exactly this. ROBOTps.x and ROBOTps.y play the role of t_x and t_y, ROBOTps.theta is theta, and LADARps.x and LADARps.y are the LiDAR's mounting location on the robot. The results are LADARxoffset and LADARyoffset, the LiDAR's position in the world frame.

Second, a for loop runs over all 228 readings, from index 0 through 227. Each object point equals the LiDAR position plus distance_ping times cosf or sinf of (that reading's angle + ROBOTps.theta). You add the robot heading because each beam angle is measured relative to the robot, not the world. Distances are in feet.

A common mistake is to forget the heading term, or to mix degrees and radians. cosf and sinf expect radians.
