6. Sensor Fusion

In the real world, motors don't spin exactly as commanded due to friction and voltage drops.

Sensors have electrical noise, mechanical imperfections, and environmental interference.

What can cause the LiDAR to fail?

  • Reflective / absorptive surfaces: glass, mirrors, black materials
  • Fog, rain, dust, snow
  • Fast motion relative to scan frequency

What can cause the odometry to fail?

  • Slippery terrain for wheel slip

When can cause the IMU fail?

  • IMU drift
  • Saturating the sensors

If we blindly execute our path plan open-loop, the robot will quickly crash.

7. Sensor Fusion

We need Localization: determining the robot's state (position, velocity, orientation) in the real world.

To solve the localization problem, SE 423 robots use multiple sensors.

Proprioceptive sensors measure internal states: Wheel Encoders (Odometry) and IMUs.

Exteroceptive sensors measure the external environment: LiDAR.

No single sensor is perfect; they all lie to us in different ways.

8. Sensor Fusion

Wheel encoders count the rotations of the robot's wheels. By knowing wheel radius and track width, we calculate forward kinematics to estimate position. It provides high-frequency, smooth data for short-term movement.

Similarly, IMUs generate high-frequency, smooth data for short-term movement based on the integration of acceleration.

LiDAR, unlike odometry and IMUs, LiDAR gives us absolute measurements relative to the environment.; however, due to the additional data, the sensor runs at a lower frequency (e.g., 10Hz) compared to an IMU (e.g., 500Hz).

If we are in a perfectly square, featureless hallway, can LiDAR tell us our position along the hallway? What about the IMU / odometry?

9. Sensor Fusion

We have fast, smooth sensors that drift (IMU, Odometry).

We have slow, computationally heavy sensors that don't drift (LiDAR).

How do we combine them?

Simple averaging doesn't work because their reliability changes over time.

If 2 sensors given measurements give different values which, should you trust?

What if your sensors give you the confidence of their measurements? I.e. the LiDAR is 75% sure of its position and the IMU / Odometry are, in total, 25% sure of their positions?

10. Sensor Fusion

Instead of saying robot is exactly at “X = 5”, we want to say “We are 90% sure that the robot is near X=5”.

We represent our state as a probability distribution.

We use the Gaussian (Normal) Distribution, defined by a mean (μ\mu) and variance (σ2\sigma^{2}).

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_distribution

11. Sensor Fusion

Why Gaussians?

The mean (μ\mu) represents our best guess of the robot's state.

The variance (σ2\sigma^{2}) represents our uncertainty; a wide curve means we are lost, a narrow curve means we are confident.

Gaussians are mathematically convenient: multiplying two Gaussians results in a new Gaussian!

In addition, a lot of math can be derived in closed form when using Gaussians (most other more complex probability distributions do not allow for this)

13. 1D Kalman Filter

14. Sensor Fusion

The Kalman Filter is an optimal estimator for linear systems subject to Gaussian noise.

When would the Kalman Filter not be appropriate?

If your sensor or system if non-linear and / or does not follow a Gaussian noise distribution! Always understand how your sensors / system acts and the underlying assumptions for algorithms!

It operates in a continuous, two-step loop: Predict and Update.

15. Sensor Fusion

16. Sensor Fusion

Step 1: The robot moves. We use our fast, noisy sensors (Odometry/IMU) to Predict where we are. Our uncertainty grows.

Step 2: The robot looks. We take a measurement with our absolute sensor (LiDAR) to Update our belief. Our uncertainty shrinks.

Rinse and repeat thousands of times a second.

20. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

We use a physical model of the robot (kinematics) and our control inputs (uu) to guess the new state.

Because we know our model and our encoders are imperfect, we actively increase our uncertainty covariance (PP).

This represents "flying blind" between reference measurements.

If we close our eyes and take 5 steps, are we more or less certain of our position than before we moved?

21. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

Before we look at massive matrices, let's look at a robot on a 1-Dimensional train track.

Its state is just one number: its position on the track, μ\mu. Its uncertainty is also just one number: the variance, σ2\sigma^{2}\ .

Together, they form a 1D Gaussian (Bell Curve) representing our "belief".

22. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

23. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

The robot commands its motors to move forward by distance uu.

Our new predicted mean simply shifts:

μpred=μold+u\mu_{pred} = \mu_{old} + u

But moving is never perfect (slipping, bumps). We add adds process noise (σmove2\sigma_{move}^{2}).

Therefore our uncertainty always grows.

σpred2=σold2+σmove2\sigma_{pred}^{2} = \sigma_{old}^{2} + \sigma_{move}^{2}

24. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

25. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

This prediction updates the covariance and the means as such because we are assuming Gaussian random variables.

Let X and Y be independent random variables that are normally distributed, then their sum is also normally distributed. i.e., if

X∼N(μX,σX2),Y∼N(μY,σY2)X \sim N\left( \mu_{X},\sigma_{X}^{2} \right),\ \ Y \sim N\left( \mu_{Y},\sigma_{Y}^{2} \right)

Z=X+YZ\ = X\ + \ Y

Then,

Z∼N(μX+μY,σX2+σY2)Z \sim N\left( \mu_{X} + \mu_{Y},\sigma_{X}^{2} + \sigma_{Y}^{2} \right)

For how to derive it you can look here (from Wikipedia)

27. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

Now, our absolute sensor (like a 1D LiDAR) takes a measurement.

It says the robot is at position zz, with a sensor noise variance of σsensor2\sigma_{sensor}^{2}.

We now have two Gaussian curves: our flattened prediction curve, and the new sensor curve.

How do we combine these two conflicting opinions into one single truth?

28. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

29. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

In probability, to fuse two independent estimates, we multiply their distributions together.

The magical property of Gaussians: Multiplying two Gaussians always produces a new Gaussian.

Because the new curve represents the overlapping area of agreement, it is always taller and narrower than both original curves.

Fusing data reduces uncertainty!

30. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

31. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

The math behind that multiplication gives us the 1D Kalman Gain (KK):

K=PredictionUncertaintyTotalSystemUncertaintyK = \frac{Prediction\ Uncertainty}{Total\ System\ Uncertainty}

If the sensor is perfect, K=1K = 1, and then we trust the sensor completely.

If the sensor is garbage, K=0K = 0, we ignore it!

32. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

The math behind that multiplication gives us the 1D Kalman Gain (KK):

K=σpred2σpred2+σsensor2K = \frac{\sigma_{pred}^{2}}{\sigma_{pred}^{2} + \sigma_{sensor}^{2}}

KK is just a ratio! It's the ratio of our prediction uncertainty to the total system uncertainty. It always falls between 0 and 1.

We update our position using this ratio:

μnew=μpred+K(z−μpred)\mu_{new} = \mu_{pred} + K\left( z - \mu_{pred} \right)

All it really says is “how much of the measurement should we incorporate”?

33. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

That’s all the Kalman filter is!

What is the current limitation of the formulation?

We are only looking at a problem with 1 state, can this be generalized to be N-dimensional?

For our differentiable robot, we live in a 2D plane with an XX, YY, and heading θ\theta.

We can no longer use a single scalar number μ\mu for position. We must use a State Vector: x=[X,Y,θ]Tx = \lbrack X,Y,\theta\rbrack^{T}.

We can no longer use a single scalar variance σ2\sigma^{2}. We must use a Covariance Matrix, PP.

36. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

37. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

Let's translate our 1D prediction (μpred=μold+u\mu_{pred} = \mu_{old} + u) to N-Dimensions:

xk|k−1=Fxk−1|k−1+Bukx_{k|k - 1} = Fx_{k - 1|k - 1} + Bu_{k}

μ\mu becomes the state vector xx

FF is our state transition matrix (how physics updates the state).

38. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

39. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

The Covariance Matrix PP represents multi-dimensional uncertainty as an ellipse, not a 1D curve.

The diagonal elements are the individual variances (e.g., how unsure are we about XX independent of everything else?).

The off-diagonal elements are the critical addition: Correlations.

If our robot only drives forward, and we are uncertain about our heading θ\theta, will that create an uncertainty in YY as we travel?

Yes, errors in θ\theta are highly correlated with errors in XX and YY.

40. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

41. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

What is Variance, Really?

Before we calculate how uncertainty grows, we must define what it is.

Imagine throwing 100 darts at a board. The Expected Value (E[X](E\lbrack X\rbrack, or mean μ\mu) is the center point of all your darts.

Variance measures how spread out those darts are from the center. It is the average of the squared distances from the mean.

We write this mathematically as:

Var(X)=E[(X−μ)2]Var(X) = E\left\lbrack (X - \mu)^{2} \right\rbrack

42. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

What happens to our uncertainty if we multiply our state by a constant aa (like applying a gear ratio to a wheel)?

Var(aX)=E[(aX−E[aX])2]Var(aX) = E\left\lbrack \left( aX - E\lbrack aX\rbrack \right)^{2} \right\rbrack

Var(aX)=E[(aX−aμ)2]Var(aX) = E\left\lbrack (aX - a\mu)^{2} \right\rbrack

Var(aX)=E[a2(X−μ)2]Var(aX) = E\left\lbrack a^{2}(X - \mu)^{2} \right\rbrack

Because a2a^{2} is a constant, we pull it out:

Var(aX)=a2E[(X−μ)2]Var(aX) = a^{2}E\left\lbrack (X - \mu)^{2} \right\rbrack

Thus, Var(aX)=a2Var(X)Var(aX) = a^{2}Var(X). Scaling the state scales the uncertainty quadratically.

If a motor spins twice as fast, does the position uncertainty double?

No, it quadruples!

43. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

Real robots experience random bumps and slip. We represent this as adding a random noise variable WW to our state.

Assuming the noise WW is completely independent of our state XX:

Var(aX+W)=Var(aX)+Var(W)Var(aX + W) = Var(aX) + Var(W)

Substituting our previous proof:

Var(aX+W)=a2Var(X)+Var(W)Var(aX + W) = a^{2}Var(X) + Var(W)

This simple 1D equation is the exact heart of the Kalman Filter's predict step!

44. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

Our 1D variance equation is Var(Xnew)=a2Var(X)+Var(W)Var\left( X_{new} \right) = a^{2}Var(X) + Var(W).

In a multidimensional robot, aa becomes the State Transition Matrix FF.

Var(X)Var(X) becomes the Covariance Matrix PP.

Var(W)Var(W) becomes the Process Noise Matrix QQ.

But how do we square a matrix to get the equivalent of a2a^{2}? We can't just write F2F^{2}!

45. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

In linear algebra, the variance of a matrix transformation is derived using the transpose:

Cov(FX)=E[(FX−E[FX])(FX−E[FX])T]=E[F(X−μ)(F(X−μ))T]Cov(FX) = E\left\lbrack \left( FX - E\lbrack FX\rbrack \right)\left( FX - E\lbrack FX\rbrack \right)^{T} \right\rbrack = E\left\lbrack F(X - \mu)\left( F(X - \mu) \right)^{T} \right\rbrack

By the rules of matrix transposition (AB)T=BTAT(AB)^{T} = B^{T}A^{T}:

=E[F(X−μ)(X−μ)TFT]= E\left\lbrack F(X - \mu)(X - \mu)^{T}F^{T} \right\rbrack

We pull the constant FF matrices out of the Expectation:

=FE[(X−μ)(X−μ)T]FT= FE\left\lbrack (X - \mu)(X - \mu)^{T} \right\rbrack F^{T}

=FPFT= FPF^{T}

Adding our noise matrix QQ, we get our final prediction: Pnew=FPFT+QP_{new} = FPF^{T} + Q.

46. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

The multi-dimensional Prediction step is thus

xk|k−1=Fxk−1|k−1+Bukx_{k|k - 1} = Fx_{k - 1|k - 1} + Bu_{k}

P=FPFT+QP\ = FPF^{T} + Q

48. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

We’ve successfully predicted our new position, but our uncertainty (PP) has grown.

Now, our sensor (e.g., LiDAR) provides a new measurement (zz).

We have two imperfect guesses for our position: our prediction and our sensor. How do we fuse them? Think of a Weighted Average. If you and an expert both guess the weight of a robot, you don't just average the two numbers.

You give more weight to the expert.

Estimate=Wmodel⋅Prediction+Wsensor⋅MeasurementEstimate = W_{model} \cdot Prediction + W_{sensor} \cdot Measurement

49. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

Again, for 1D the equation is:

μnew=μpred+K(z−μpred)\mu_{new} = \mu_{pred} + K\left( z - \mu_{pred} \right)

Where we have:

K=σpred2σpred2+σsensor2K = \frac{\sigma_{pred}^{2}}{\sigma_{pred}^{2} + \sigma_{sensor}^{2}}

If our sensor is perfect (σsensor2=0\sigma_{sensor}^{2} = 0), what does KK become, and what happens to μnew\mu_{new}?

K=1K\ = \ 1, and our new estimate exactly equals the sensor measurement!

50. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

In 1D, state and measurement were both just position along a track. In 2D/3D, our state is x=[X,Y,θ]Tx = \lbrack X,Y,\theta\rbrack^{T}. But our LiDAR might just measure the straight-line distance to a wall.

What is the problem?

We cannot subtract a state vector from a distance measurement! They speak different languages!

We introduce the Observation Matrix (HH), which translates our internal state into the format of the sensor.

Expected Measurement = HxpredHx_{pred}

51. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

We thus, start by modifying the 1D Kalman Gain into matrices.

K=σpred2σpred2+σsensor2K = \frac{\sigma_{pred}^{2}}{\sigma_{pred}^{2} + \sigma_{sensor}^{2}}

σpred2\sigma_{pred}^{2} becomes our covariance PP.

σsensor2\sigma_{sensor}^{2} becomes our measurement noise RR.

But we must map PP into sensor space using HH. Just like FPFTFPF^{T} for motion, we use HPHTHPH^{T} for sensors!

52. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

We thus, start by modifying the 1D Kalman Gain into matrices.

K=σpred2σpred2+σsensor2K = \frac{\sigma_{pred}^{2}}{\sigma_{pred}^{2} + \sigma_{sensor}^{2}}

The numerator is the prediction covariance mapped to the sensor. The denominator is the total system noise.

Because we can't divide matrices, we multiply by the inverse:

K=PHT(HPHT+R)−1K = PH^{T}\left( HPH^{T} + R \right)^{- 1}

53. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

54. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

Now we apply our matrix KK to update the multi-dimensional state.

The 1D equation μnew=μpred+K(z−μpred)\mu_{new} = \mu_{pred} + K\left( z - \mu_{pred} \right) becomes:

xk|k=xk|k−1+K(zk−Hxk|k−1)x_{k|k} = x_{k|k - 1} + K\left( z_{k} - Hx_{k|k - 1} \right)

Finally, we update the uncertainty ellipse. We pull the information from the sensor out of our uncertainty:

Pkk=(I−KH)Pkk−1P_{kk} = (I - KH)P_{kk - 1}

(II is the Identity Matrix, the matrix equivalent of the number 1).

And we are done!

55. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

56. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

But what does (I−KH)(I - KH) mean physically?

Think of the Identity Matrix (II) as the number 1, representing 100% of our previous uncertainty (Pk|k−1P_{k|k - 1}).

HH maps our state to the sensor.

KK is how much we trust the sensor.

Therefore, KHKH is the percentage of uncertainty the sensor measurement removes.

If the sensor is completely useless (pure noise, K=0), what does the equation become?

Pnew=(I−0)Pold=PoldP_{new} = (I - 0)P_{old} = P_{old}. Our uncertainty doesn't shrink at all!

57. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

Prediction:

State Prediction (Where do we think we are?):

xk|k−1=Fxk−1|k−1+Bukx_{k|k - 1} = Fx_{k - 1|k - 1} + Bu_{k}

Covariance Prediction (How uncertain are we getting?):

Pk|k−1=FPk−1|k−1FT+QP_{k|k - 1} = FP_{k - 1|k - 1}F^{T} + Q

Update:

The Kalman Gain (Who do we trust more?):

K=Pk|k−1HT(HPk|k−1HT+R)−1K = P_{k|k - 1}H^{T}\left( HP_{k|k - 1}H^{T} + R \right)^{- 1}

State Update (Correcting our position):

xk|k=xk|k−1+K(zk−Hxk|k−1)x_{k|k} = x_{k|k - 1} + K\left( z_{k} - Hx_{k|k - 1} \right)

Covariance Update (Shrinking our uncertainty):

Pk|k=(I−KH)Pk|k−1P_{k|k} = (I - KH)P_{k|k - 1}

59. Optional Derivations

60. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

To derive the multi-dimensional math, we must define our ultimate goal: Minimizing Uncertainty.

Mathematically, total uncertainty is the sum of the variances of our state variables (the diagonal elements of the covariance matrix PP).

The sum of the diagonal elements of a matrix is called the Trace, written Tr(P)Tr(P).

Thus the objective is to find the Gain matrix, that forces the derive to achieve:

∂Tr(Pk|k)∂K=0\frac{\partial Tr\left( P_{k|k} \right)}{\partial K} = 0

61. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

Let's define the actual error of our updated state: e=xtrue−xk|ke = x_{true} - x_{k|k}.

We substitute our update equation xk|k=xk|k−1+K(z−Hxk|k−1)x_{k|k} = x_{k|k - 1} + K\left( z - Hx_{k|k - 1} \right) into the error definition.

Covariance is defined as the expected value of the error squared: Pk|k=E[eeT]P_{k|k} = E\left\lbrack ee^{T} \right\rbrack.

Expanding this massive expected value yields the universally true Joseph Form of the covariance update:

Pk|k=(I−KH)Pk|k−1(I−KH)T+KRKTP_{k|k} = (I - KH)P_{k|k - 1}(I - KH)^{T} + KRK^{T}

Why do we see both PP (model noise) and RR (sensor noise) in this equation?

Because our new estimate relies on both, so both sources contribute to the final error

62. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

We have our covariance equation. Now, we use matrix calculus to minimize its Trace:

∂Tr(Pk|k)∂K=0\frac{\partial Tr\left( P_{k|k} \right)}{\partial K} = 0

Taking the derivative of the Joseph Form with respect to KK yields:

−2(I−KH)Pk|k−1HT+2KR=0- 2(I - KH)P_{k|k - 1}H^{T} + 2KR = 0

Divide by 2, expand, and isolate the KK terms:

Pk|k−1HT=K(HPk|k−1HT+R)P_{k|k - 1}H^{T} = K\left( HP_{k|k - 1}H^{T} + R \right)There it is!

The Pkk−1HTP_{kk - 1}H^{T} term emerges naturally from taking the derivative of the covariance. It represents the cross-covariance between our state error and our measurement error.

63. Sensor Fusion – Predict Step

Now we have our optimal KK. But calculating the massive Joseph Form

Pk|k=(I−KH)Pk|k−1(I−KH)T+KRKTP_{k|k} = (I - KH)P_{k|k - 1}(I - KH)^{T} + KRK^{T}

takes too much processing power on a microcontroller. Let's substitute our newly derived optimal KK back into the Joseph Form.

Because K(HPHT+R)=PHTK\left( HPH^{T} + R \right) = PH^{T}, massive sections of the Joseph equation perfectly cancel each other out.

The equation collapses into the short form:

Pk|k=(I−KH)Pk|k−1P_{k|k} = (I - KH)P_{k|k - 1}

Up to this point, we have looked at a lot of theoretical matrices: F,H,P,Q,RF,\ H,\ P,\ Q,\ R

But that is always difficult to understand. We will build a complete, multi-dimensional Kalman Filter from scratch for a simple robotic system!

67. Kalman Filter Example

Imagine a simple robot constrained to a 1D track (like a train). It can move forward and backward, but it cannot steer.

We want to track both its Position (pp) and its Velocity (vv).

What would our state be?

Our State Vector (xx) is now a 2x1 column matrix:

x=[pv]x = \begin{bmatrix} p \\ v \end{bmatrix}

68. Kalman Filter Example

To predict the future state, we need basic kinematic equations.

If our time step is Δt\Delta t, the new position is the old position plus velocity times time:

pnew=pold+voldΔtp_{new} = p_{old} + v_{old}\Delta t

Assuming a constant velocity model (for simplicity), the new velocity is just the old velocity:

vnew=voldv_{new} = v_{old}

75. Extended Kalman Filter

We use differential drive platforms operating in a 2D plane in lab.

The state vector is x=[X,Y,θ]Tx = \lbrack X,Y,\theta\rbrack^{T}.

The physics equations for the next time step (dtdt) involve steering:

Xnew=Xold+vcos⁡(θ)dtX_{new} = X_{old} + v\cos(\theta)dt

What is the problem?

You cannot create a constant linear matrix FF that multiplies by θ\theta to produce cos⁡(θ)\cos(\theta).

76. Extended Kalman Filter

The standard Kalman Filter guarantees an optimal estimate if and only if the system remains Gaussian.

If you pass a Gaussian distribution through a linear function (like 2x2x), it stays Gaussian!

If you pass a Gaussian distribution through a highly non-linear function (like sine or cosine), it becomes warped, skewed, and destroys the assumptions of the filter!

79. Extended Kalman Filter

To solve this, we introduce the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF)

The EKF uses the Taylor Series expansion to approximate the non-linear curves as flat lines right at our current operating point.

By zooming in incredibly close to the robot's current state, the curve looks like a straight line.

We use that temporary straight line (tangent) to update our uncertainty.

What calculus tool allows us to find these tangents?

Derivates (in this case Partial Derivatives)

81. Extended Kalman Filter

To find that linear approximation, we take the derivative.

Because our state has multiple variables (X,Y,θX,\ Y,\ \theta), we must take the Partial Derivative of every output equation with respect to every input state variable.

We arrange these partial derivatives into a grid called the Jacobian Matrix.

F=[∂f1∂X∂f1∂Y∂f1∂θ∂f2∂X∂f2∂Y∂f2∂θ∂3∂X∂f3∂Y∂f3∂θ]F\, = \,\begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial X} & \frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial Y} & \frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial\theta} \\ \frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial X} & \frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial Y} & \frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\theta} \\ \frac{\partial 3}{\partial X} & \frac{\partial f_{3}}{\partial Y} & \frac{\partial f_{3}}{\partial\theta} \end{bmatrix}

In the EKF, the dynamic Jacobian matrix replaces the static linear FF matrix.

82. Extended Kalman Filter

Let's calculate the partial derivatives for our XX position equation: f1=X+vcos⁡(θ)dtf_{1} = X + v\cos(\theta)dt.

With respect to XX:

∂f1∂X=1\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial X} = 1

With respect to YY:

∂f1∂Y=0\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial Y} = 0

With respect to θ\theta:

We take the derivative of vcos⁡(θ)dtv\cos(\theta)dt. The derivative of cos⁡(⋅)\cos{( \cdot )} is −sin⁡(⋅)- \sin{( \cdot )}.

∂f1∂θ=−vsin⁡(θ)dt\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial\theta} = - v\sin(\theta)dt

83. Extended Kalman Filter

Let's calculate the partial derivatives for our YY position equation: f2=Y+vsin⁡(θ)dtf_{2} = Y + v\sin(\theta)dt.

With respect to XX:

∂f2∂X=0\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial X} = 0

With respect to YY:

∂f2∂Y=1\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial Y} = 1

With respect to θ\theta:

We derive vsin⁡(θ)dtv\sin(\theta)dt. The derivative of sin⁡(⋅)\sin{( \cdot )} is cos⁡(⋅)\cos{\ ( \cdot )}.

∂f2∂θ=vcos⁡(θ)dt\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\theta} = v\cos(\theta)dt

84. Extended Kalman Filter

Putting everything together into the State Transition Matrix:

Fk=[10−vksin⁡(θk)dt01vkcos⁡(θk)dt001]F_{k} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & - v_{k}\sin\left( \theta_{k} \right)dt \\ 0 & 1 & v_{k}\cos\left( \theta_{k} \right)dt \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}

Notice the kk subscript. This matrix is no longer static.

You must re-calculate FkF_{k} on your microcontroller every single loop based on your current velocity and heading!

91. Extended Kalman Filter

Let's say our robot is being tracked by a stationary radar tower located at the origin (0,0)(0,0)

The radar doesn't measure XX and YY coordinates. It outputs a measurement vector zz containing two values: distance (rr) and bearing angle (ϕ\phi).

z=[rϕ]z = \begin{bmatrix} r \\ \phi \end{bmatrix}

92. Extended Kalman Filter

During the Predict Step, we need to guess what the radar should see based on our internal belief of where we are (try to guess the radar measurement).

We use geometry to map our state space into sensor space. This is our non-linear measurement function, h(x)h(x):

r=X2+Y2r = \sqrt{X^{2} + Y^{2}}

ϕ=arctan⁡(YX)\phi = \arctan\left( \frac{Y}{X} \right)

Just like kinematics, we cannot express this as a linear matrix multiplication. We must use Jacobians to linearize it!

93. Extended Kalman Filter

We must take the partial derivatives of rr and ϕ\phi with respect to our state variables (X,Y,θX,\ Y,\ \theta).

Let's look at the distance equation: r=X2+Y2r = \sqrt{X^{2} + Y^{2}}

Using the chain rule:

With respect to XX: ∂r∂X=XX2+Y2=Xr\frac{\partial r}{\partial X} = \frac{X}{\sqrt{X^{2} + Y^{2}}} = \frac{X}{r}

With respect to Y:∂r∂Y=YX2+Y2=YrY:\ \frac{\partial r}{\partial Y} = \frac{Y}{\sqrt{X^{2} + Y^{2}}} = \frac{Y}{r}

With respect to θ:∂r∂θ=0\theta:\ \frac{\partial r}{\partial\theta} = 0

94. Extended Kalman Filter

Now the bearing equation: ϕ=arctan⁡(Y/X)\phi = \arctan\left( Y\text{/}X \right).

The derivative of arctan⁡(u)is11+u2⋅u′\arctan(u)is\frac{1}{1 + u^{2}} \cdot u'.

After some algebra:

With respect to XX: ∂ϕ∂X=−YX2+Y2=−Yr2\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial X} = - \frac{Y}{X^{2} + Y^{2}} = - \frac{Y}{r^{2}}

With respect to YY: ∂ϕ∂Y=XX2+Y2=Xr2\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial Y} = \frac{X}{X^{2} + Y^{2}} = \frac{X}{r^{2}}

With respect to θ\theta: ∂ϕ∂θ=0\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta} = 0

95. Extended Kalman Filter

We assemble our partial derivatives into a 2×32\ \times 3 matrix (2 measurement outputs, 3 state variables).

This is our dynamic HkH_{k} matrix for the current time step:

Hk=[XrYr0−Yr2Xr20]H_{k} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{X}{r} & \frac{Y}{r} & 0 \\ & & \\ - \frac{Y}{r^{2}} & \frac{X}{r^{2}} & 0 \end{bmatrix}

We calculate this exact matrix every single loop using our predicted XX and YY values.

96. Extended Kalman Filter

Now that we have our non-linear h(x)h(x) and our linear Jacobian HkH_{k}, we execute the EKF Update step:

1. Calculate Kalman Gain:

Kk=Pkk−1HkT(HkPkk−1HkT+R)−1K_{k} = P_{kk - 1}H_{k}^{T}\left( H_{k}P_{kk - 1}H_{k}^{T} + R \right)^{- 1}

xk|k=xk|k−1+Kk(zmeasured−[X2+Y2arctan⁡(Y/X)])x_{k|k} = x_{k|k - 1} + K_{k}\left( z_{measured} - \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{X^{2} + Y^{2}} \\ \arctan\left( Y\text{/}X \right) \end{bmatrix} \right)

Pkk=(I−KkHk)Pkk−1P_{kk} = \left( I - K_{k}H_{k} \right)P_{kk - 1}
