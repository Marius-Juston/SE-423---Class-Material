After the discussion, check your answers. In RGB, brightness is mixed into all three channels, so a fixed color threshold breaks when the lighting changes. Lab separates lightness, L*, from color: a* runs from green to red and b* runs from blue to yellow. In a shadow, L* drops, while a* (still strongly positive for red) and b* stay roughly the same. That is why you threshold on a* and b*.

For the centroid, M00 = sum of all blob pixels, which is the area. M10 = sum of x over those pixels, and M01 = sum of y. Then c_x = M10/M00 and c_y = M01/M00. A common mistake is forgetting to divide by M00.

The one-eye experiment leads into today's topic. A single eye, like a single camera, projects 3D onto 2D and loses depth. Two views recover it, and that is where this lecture on camera geometry and stereo vision begins.

Cameras map the 3D world into a 2D image plane.

Is this a lossy or lossless transformation? If lossy give an example.

Infinite points on a single ray maps to the exact same pixel.

However, we would like to control robots in 3D space, as such we must invert the projection.

If infinite 3D points map to one pixel, is the inverse function mathematically solvable with a single image?

Lecture notes

This slide opens the Camera Geometry section with a short chain of statements and two questions, all text, no figures. It states that cameras map the 3D world into a 2D image plane, then asks whether that transformation is lossy or lossless, and if lossy, for an example. The centered answer is that infinite points on a single ray map to the exact same pixel. It continues: we want to control robots in 3D space, so we must invert the projection, and the first step is a model of the camera. It ends by asking whether the inverse is mathematically solvable from a single image.

Before reading the answer line, pause and let the class discuss. The projection is lossy because it throws away depth. Picture a ray leaving the camera through one pixel. A coin 10 cm away and a dinner plate 1 m away on that same ray can land on exactly the same pixels. Once the light hits the sensor, nothing in that pixel value tells you which distance it came from.

So the answer to the last question is no. A single image gives you a direction, the ray, but not a range along it. To recover 3D you need extra information. That can be known object size, as with AprilTags, an assumption such as the object sitting on the floor, a second view from stereo or a moving camera, or a learned prior from a depth network. Those are exactly the topics of the rest of this lecture.

For your robot this means one camera alone cannot tell you how far away an obstacle is without one of these extra cues.

https://visionbook.mit.edu/imaging.html#fig-orthographics

Lecture notes

This slide shows a concrete example of the 3D to 2D projection we just discussed. The figure comes from the MIT Foundations of Computer Vision book, at visionbook.mit.edu/imaging.html#fig-orthographics.

On the right, an L-shaped step block, like two stairs, sits along the world Z axis. Its front faces are orange, its top faces green, and its end face blue. The world frame has Y pointing up, X pointing diagonally, and Z running horizontally to the right. On the left is a gray rectangle labeled "Projection plane" with green image axes, lowercase x and y. Dashed lines run from each corner of the block straight back along Z, all parallel to each other, and hit the plane. There the block appears as an L-shaped outline of exactly the same size.

This is orthographic projection. The mapping is simply x = X and y = Y: image x equals world X, image y equals world Y, and Z is thrown away. Because the rays are parallel, moving the block farther along Z does not change its size in the image. This makes the loss of information from the last slide very visible. Every point along one dashed line lands on the same image point, so depth cannot be recovered from this single view.

Real cameras do not behave this way. In a real camera, distant objects look smaller. Orthographic projection is a useful approximation only when an object's depth range is small compared with its distance from the camera.

Next, we replace these parallel rays with rays that all pass through a single point. That gives the pinhole camera model.

10. The Pinhole Camera Model (1/24)

A pinhole camera projects 3D coordinates in the world to 2D positions on the projection plane of the camera through the straight-line path of each light ray through the pinhole.

There are 3 coordinate systems that we will use.

Lecture notes

This slide introduces the pinhole camera model. It has only text, no figure. The first statement: a pinhole camera projects 3D coordinates in the world to 2D positions on the camera's projection plane, following the straight-line path of each light ray through the pinhole. The second statement: there are 3 coordinate systems that we will use.

On the previous slide you saw orthographic projection. There, parallel rays carry each point straight back to the image plane, so image x equals world X, image y equals world Y, and depth is simply discarded. Real cameras do not work that way. In a pinhole camera every ray from the scene passes through one single point, the pinhole, before it lands on the projection plane. This one geometric fact explains perspective. Objects farther away appear smaller because their rays pass through the pinhole at a shallower angle.

The model also answers the question from slide 8. Every 3D point along one ray through the pinhole lands on the same 2D spot. A single image therefore tells you the direction to a point, but not its distance. Keep that in mind when you later try to locate objects for the robot from camera images.

The "straight-line path" is the key phrase. Because rays are straight lines, the projection reduces to similar triangles, and that gives clean equations.

Next, we build the three coordinate systems one at a time. The first is a world frame centered at the pinhole. Then come coordinates on the projection plane, and finally the pixel coordinates you actually read from an image.

This slide introduces the first of the three coordinate systems from the previous slide: world coordinates. The diagram on the right shows the pinhole camera as a box. Camera coordinates in the virtual camera plane.

The camera projection plane is behind the pinhole and at a distance ff of the origin.

Because the rays get inverted through the pinhole, it is useful to reflect the project plane so that the coordinates are easier to understand

https://visionbook.mit.edu/imaging.html#fig-orthographics

Lecture notes

This slide introduces the second of our three coordinate systems: camera coordinates, which live in what we call the virtual camera plane. The text says the real projection plane sits behind the pinhole, a distance f from the origin, and that because rays are inverted as they pass through the pinhole, it is useful to reflect that plane so the coordinates are easier to understand.

The diagram is the same one as on the last slide. The camera is drawn as a box. Its back face is the projection plane, and its front face holds the pinhole, or camera center. The pinhole is the origin of the world axes: Y points up, X points diagonally, and Z points out of the camera along the optical, or principal, axis. A red arrow labels the box as the pinhole camera. The depth of the box, from the pinhole back to the projection plane, is marked f, the focal length.

Here is why we reflect the plane. Every ray from a scene point travels in a straight line through the pinhole. A point above the optical axis therefore lands below it on the back plane, and left and right swap too, so the physical image is upside down. If you imagine a copy of that plane a distance f in front of the pinhole, at Z = f, the same rays cross it with the image upright. The geometry and the similar triangles are identical, and you lose an awkward minus sign.

A common mistake is to think the virtual plane is physical. The real sensor is still behind the lens. The virtual plane is a mathematical convenience.

Next, we measure positions on this plane in pixels, which gives us image coordinates.

13. The Pinhole Camera Model (4/24)

Image coordinates, are typically measured in pixels.

Both the camera coordinate system (x,y)(x,y) and the image coordinate system (n,m)(n,\ m) are related by an affine transform as we will discuss later.

https://visionbook.mit.edu/imaging.html#fig-orthographics

Lecture notes

This slide adds the third and last coordinate system of the pinhole model: image coordinates. The text says image coordinates are typically measured in pixels, and that the camera coordinate system, (x, y), and the image coordinate system, (n, m), are related by an affine transform, which the lecture covers later. The diagram is the same one from the last two slides: a box-shaped pinhole camera with the projection plane at its back face and the pinhole, or camera center, on its front face. The pinhole is the origin of the world X, Y, Z axes, with Z along the optical, or principal, axis, and the focal length f is the distance from the pinhole back to the projection plane.

Here is why you need a third system. Camera coordinates (x, y) on the virtual plane are physical distances, in the same length units as f, measured from the center of the plane. Your code never sees those. It sees a pixel array indexed by integers (n, m), counted from a corner.

An affine transform is a linear map plus a shift. Here it does three things. It scales physical length into pixels, using the pixel size of the sensor. It translates the origin from the plane's center to a corner of the image. It can also flip an axis when the directions disagree. On the next slide, notice that x points left while n and m start at the bottom-left corner.

A common mistake is mixing these units, for example plugging a focal length in millimeters into a pixel computation. Quick check: if the optical axis hits the exact center of a 640 by 480 image, what pixel does camera point (0, 0) map to?

15. The Pinhole Camera Model (5/24)

The pinhole model assumes the light passes through a single infinitively small aperture.

By similar triangles, a 3D point (X,Y,Z)(X,Y,Z), projects to the image plane at (x,y)(x,y).

The ideal projection equations are:

x=fXZ,y=fYZx = f\frac{X}{Z},\ \ y = f\frac{Y}{Z}

Where ff, represents the focal length.

These equations are called the perspective projection equations.

Lecture notes

This slide is text only, with the two key equations centered. It states that the pinhole model assumes all light passes through a single, infinitely small aperture. By similar triangles, a 3D point (X, Y, Z) projects onto the image plane at (x, y). The ideal projection equations are x = f*X/Z, read "x equals f times X over Z," and y = f*Y/Z, read "y equals f times Y over Z," where f is the focal length. These are called the perspective projection equations.

This puts numbers on the diagram from the last slide. Every ray from a 3D point passes through the pinhole, so the triangle formed by the point, the pinhole and the optical axis is similar to the triangle formed by the image point, the pinhole and the image plane. The ratios of the sides must match: x/f = X/Z and y/f = Y/Z. Multiply both sides by f and you get the equations on the slide. We use the virtual image plane in front of the pinhole, so there is no minus sign from the inverted image.

Try a quick example. With f = 500 pixels, a point 1 m to the side at depth Z = 2 m lands at x = 500*1/2 = 250 pixels. Move it back to Z = 4 m and it lands at 125 pixels. The division by Z means depth is lost: every point along the same ray maps to the same pixel. That is why we will need stereo later in the lecture to recover depth.

A common mistake is to plug in world coordinates. X, Y and Z must be measured in the camera frame, with Z along the optical axis.

Next, we look at the similar-triangle figure and see why dividing by Z makes distant objects smaller.

16. The Pinhole Camera Model (6/24)

Under perspective projection, distant objects become smaller, through the inverse scaling by ZZ.

The perspective projection equations apply not just to pinhole cameras but to most lens-based cameras, and human vision as well. The pixel coordinated can be computed as,

n=−ax+n0,m=ay+m0n = - ax + n_{0},\ \ m = ay + m_{0}

Lecture notes

This slide finishes the basic pinhole model by turning the projection equations into actual pixel locations.

The top line says that under perspective projection, distant objects become smaller through the inverse scaling by Z. The figure shows why. A gray image plane sits on the left, and the camera center, the pinhole, is at the origin of three black axes. Z, the optical axis, points to the right into the scene. A red point in the world, at coordinates X, Y, Z, connects by a dotted line through the center to a brown point on the image plane a focal distance f behind it, so the image is flipped. Two pairs of similar triangles carry the geometry: green triangles for the horizontal coordinate and blue triangles for the vertical one. They give x / f = X / Z and y / f = Y / Z, so x = f*X/Z and y = f*Y/Z. Because Z is in the denominator, if you double an object's distance, its image size halves.

The slide then notes that these perspective projection equations hold not only for pinhole cameras but for most lens-based cameras, and for human vision too. The camera coordinates x and y are physical lengths on the image plane, so we still need pixels: n = -a*x + n_0 and m = a*y + m_0. Here a is the number of pixels per unit length, assumed the same in both directions (square pixels). The pair (n_0, m_0) is the pixel location of the image center. The minus sign appears because, as on slide 14, camera x points left while the pixel index n increases to the right from the bottom-left corner. Dropping that sign is a common mistake.

Quick check: where does a point on the optical axis land in pixels? It lands at (n_0, m_0).

The division by Z makes these equations nonlinear, which motivates the homogeneous coordinates on the next slide.

17. The Pinhole Camera Model (7/24)

Homogeneous coordinates represent a vector of length NN by a vector of length N+1N\ + \ 1. The transformation rule from heterogeneous to homogeneous coordinates is simply to add an additional coordinate, 1, to the heterogeneous coordinates as shown here:

(xy)→[xy1]\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} \rightarrow \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}

To go from homogeneous coordinates back to the heterogeneous representation of the point, we divide all the entries of the homogeneous coordinate vector by their last dimension

[xyw]→(x/wy/w)\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ w \end{bmatrix} \rightarrow \begin{pmatrix} x/w \\ y/w \end{pmatrix}

Lecture notes

This slide introduces homogeneous coordinates, the tool that lets us write the perspective projection from the last two slides as a single matrix multiplication. This slide introduces the second way to measure distance to the boat: triangulation. Where the previous slide used a single elevated observer and the angle below the horizon, here you use two observation points on the coast, separated by a known distance t, called the baseline.

2. Divide by the length of its first column so that r1 has unit length.

4. Read t from the third column.

The equation is v = M*P_infinity = K[R T][D; 0] = K*R*D. In words: v equals the camera matrix M times P-infinity. That equals the intrinsic matrix K times the extrinsic matrix [R | T], times the stacked vector of the direction D over a zero, which simplifies to K times R times D. The slide concludes that vanishing points do not depend on camera translation, and only a camera rotation moves them. In bold, it adds that two identical cameras translated from each other produce images with the same vanishing points.

On the last slide you saw that each family of parallel 3D lines converges to its own vanishing point. Here is why. A point infinitely far along direction D has homogeneous coordinates [D; 0], and the zero fourth component means "direction, not location." When you multiply by [R | T], the translation column T is multiplied by that zero and drops out. You are left with R*D, the direction rotated into the camera frame, and K maps it to pixels. To get pixel coordinates, divide v by its third component. Forgetting this step is a common mistake.

"Identical" matters here. Both cameras need the same K and the same orientation R, so the only difference between them is T. That is exactly the rectified stereo setup.

Lecture notes

This slide is text only. It makes four points. First, just as parallel 3D lines converge to the same vanishing point, parallel 3D planes share the same horizon line. Second, if lines l_1 and l_2 in homogeneous coordinates are the image projections of two parallel 3D lines, their vanishing point is their intersection, computed as the cross product l_1 × l_2 ("l one cross l two"). Third, a second set of parallel lines in the same plane gives a second vanishing point the same way. Fourth, the horizon line is the line through both vanishing points, which is also a cross product, this time of the two points.

The key fact is that in homogeneous coordinates, points and lines in the image are both 3-vectors. A line l = (a, b, c) is the set of pixels (u, v, 1) with a*u + b*v + c = 0. Two rules follow. The intersection of two lines is p = l_1 × l_2, and the line through two points is l = p_1 × p_2. To get a line from two detected edge pixels, cross their homogeneous coordinates.

So the whole recipe is three cross products: h = (l_1 × l_2) × (l_3 × l_4). Notice that no division is needed until the end. That matters because a vanishing point can lie far outside the image, as in the plaza photo on the last slide. If the image lines are exactly parallel, the third coordinate of the vanishing point is zero: a point at infinity.

A common mistake is to normalize by that third coordinate too early and divide by nearly zero. Keep the full 3-vector until you actually need pixel values.

Quick check: why does the floor of a room and a tabletop share one horizon line? Because the two planes are parallel, they share the same vanishing points.

Before reading pixel coordinates, divide each point by its third component. If that component is near zero, the image lines are almost parallel and the vanishing point is near infinity. The method also requires both families to be parallel in 3D and to lie in the same plane, or the line you get is not that plane's horizon.

This slide turns the question around. The previous slide listed what perspective projection destroys: angles, relative lengths, and parallelism. Here is why this matters. A camera maps a 3D line to a 2D line, so points that are collinear in the world stay collinear in the image. Distances along that line get stretched or squashed by different amounts, though, so a simple ratio like |P1P2| / |P2P3| changes from one viewpoint to another. Lecture notes

This slide gives the formal definition of the cross-ratio, the quantity the previous slide introduced as one of the few things perspective projection preserves.

The equation reads: the cross-ratio of P1, P2, P3, P4 equals the distance from P1 to P3 times the distance from P2 to P4, divided by the distance from P2 to P3 times the distance from P1 to P4. In plain text: CR(P1, P2, P3, P4) = (|P3 − P1| * |P4 − P2|) / (|P3 − P2| * |P4 − P1|). Each |Pi − Pj| is the ordinary Euclidean distance between two points. The slide calls these heterogeneous coordinates. It means the usual non-homogeneous coordinates: if your image points are in homogeneous form (x, y, w), divide by w first so you are measuring actual pixel positions.

The figure shows a point O on the left with four lines radiating from it. Three red transversal lines cut those four rays. One nearly vertical line crosses them at P1 to P4. A slanted line crosses them at Q1 to Q4. A third line near the top crosses them at R4, R3 and R2. It is parallel to the bottom ray, so R1 is marked with an arrow to infinity. The point of the figure is that all three sets of four points have the same cross-ratio, even though their spacings look very different.

"The order matters" means that relabeling the points changes the value. For example, swapping P1 and P2 turns the ratio into its reciprocal, 1/CR. Always keep the points in the same order along the line in every view you compare.

Lecture notes

This slide finishes the cross-ratio example by handling the special case where one of the four points lies at infinity. This is exactly why vanishing points are useful. In an image, a vanishing point is the image of a point at infinity. The cross-ratio lets you relate image distances along a line to true distances, using only a few known positions.

Lecture notes

This slide is text only, with four short statements that set up the problem we solve with the cross-ratio. First, the big problem: how do you measure the height of one object given the known height of a reference object in the same scene? Second, because of perspective projection, you cannot compare heights directly using distances measured in the image. Fourth, the procedure: find common points, compute the ground plane, find the vanishing point on the horizon line, get the intersecting point, and measure the height relative to the reference.

Why can't you just take a ratio of pixel lengths? An object farther from the camera looks shorter. Even along a single vertical line, equal 3D lengths do not map to equal pixel lengths, because spacing shrinks toward the vanishing point. A common mistake is to treat the image as if it were drawn to scale.

Putting the objects in correspondence means moving the unknown height onto the reference object's vertical line. Both objects stand on the ground plane. Draw the line through their two ground contact points and extend it until it meets the horizon line, at a vanishing point. Every line through that vanishing point is parallel in 3D to the ground line. So the line from the vanishing point through the top of the unknown object hits the reference's vertical edge at exactly the unknown height. Now both lengths lie on one line, and the cross-ratio from the last two slides, with the vertical vanishing point as the point at infinity, turns image distances into a true height.
